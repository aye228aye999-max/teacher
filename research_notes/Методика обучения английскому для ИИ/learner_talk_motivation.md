## 1. Maximising learner talk: teacher vs student talk time, elicitation, display vs referential questions, wait time, IRF, scaffolding, negotiation of meaning

### Takeaway
Interaction that makes learners produce and negotiate meaning reliably helps acquisition. The effects are large in meta-analysis, especially for vocabulary, and hold up on delayed tests. What matters most is **how** the teacher's turns open or close space for the learner, more than how much the teacher talks:
- **Open learner space:** referential (genuine) questions, longer wait time, content-based follow-up, clarification requests, scaffolding, and prompts that push self-repair.
- **Close it down:** evaluative closure ("Very good!"), teacher echo, finishing the learner's sentence, interruptions, and display questions.

### Cited Findings

**A. Strong empirical evidence (meta-analyses and experiments)**
- [META] **Interaction effects.** Mackey & Goo (2007) meta-analysed 28 interaction studies run inside and outside classrooms.
  - Learners who took part in interaction showed **large effects** compared with learners who did not.
  - The effects were clearer on **delayed** posttests.
  - The effects were **larger for lexis than grammar**.
  - Sources: [Lancaster University record](https://research.lancaster-university.uk/en/publications/interaction-research-in-sla-a-meta-analysis-and-research-synthesi/); summarised in the [Loewen & Sato (2018) review in *Language Teaching*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/interaction-and-instructed-second-language-acquisition/78A156EE200F744F5978F99BFB073DBE).
- [META] **Oral corrective feedback.** Lyster & Saito (2010), *SSLA* 32(2): 265–302, covered 15 classroom studies with 827 participants.
  - Corrective feedback had **significant and durable** effects.
  - **Prompts** (feedback that pushes the learner to self-repair) had larger effects than **recasts** (teacher reformulations).
  - Effects were **most apparent on free, constructed-response measures**, the closest to spontaneous speech.
  - Younger learners benefited more than older ones.
  - Long treatments beat short-to-medium ones, but could not be distinguished from brief ones.
  - Instructional setting did not matter.
  - Sources: [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1); [McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/concern/articles/6682x769r); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ892626).
- [META] [unverified] **Corrective feedback, second meta-analysis.** Li (2010), *Language Learning* 60(2), 33 studies.
  - Medium overall effect (reported d ≈ 0.61), maintained over time.
  - Explicit feedback was stronger short-term; implicit feedback was more durable.
  - [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x)
- [CA, 1997] [unverified] **Recasts vs uptake.** Lyster & Ranta (1997), *SSLA* 19(1), French immersion classrooms.
  - Recasts were the most frequent teacher feedback (about half of all feedback moves) but the **least likely to lead to learner uptake**.
  - Elicitation, metalinguistic clues, clarification requests and repetition led to more **student-generated repair**.
  - [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263197001034)
- [EXP, 2008] [unverified] **Anxiety and recasts.** Sheen (2008), *Language Learning* 58(4): recasts helped **low-anxiety** learners (more modified output and larger accuracy gains) but did not help **high-anxiety** learners. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2008.00480.x)
- [QEXP, small, 1986] **Referential questions.** Brock (1986), *TESOL Quarterly* 20(1): 47–59, with 4 experienced ESL teachers and 24 adult learners.
  - The 2 teachers trained to ask more referential questions (whose answers the teacher does not know) got learner answers that were **significantly longer and more syntactically complex, with more connectives**.
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.2307/3586388); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ334474).
- [Observational, L1 science classrooms, 1986] **Wait time.** Rowe (1986), *Journal of Teacher Education* 37(1).
  - Teachers typically wait **under 1 second** after a question ("wait time 1") and under 1 second after a student answer ("wait time 2").
  - Extending wait time to **3 seconds or more** made answers **300–700% longer**, with more inference and speculation and more student-to-student exchange.
  - Sources: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002248718603700110); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ333700); secondary summaries at [Faculty Focus](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/effective-teaching-strategies/student-learning-in-3-seconds/) and [Tips for Teachers](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/research-wait-time/).
- [CA/dynamic-systems study, L2, 2017] **Wait time in an L2 classroom.** Smith & King (2017), *System* 68: 1–14, Japanese university EFL.
  - Wait time on its own **reinforced the IRF pattern and limited student-initiated talk**.
  - **Longer individual student responses**, not just more responses, improved discourse quality.
  - Waits **over 2 seconds** occasionally shifted talk out of IRF for a while.
  - [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X17304608). A follow-up by the same authors on post-elicitation wait time appeared in 2025 (content not accessed): [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/00336882241280987).
- [EXP] [unverified] **Task repetition and fluency.**
  - Nation (1989), *System* 17(3), the **4/3/2** technique: the same talk given to new listeners in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes increased fluency (faster speech, fewer hesitations), with some gains in accuracy and complexity. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0346-251X(89)90010-9)
  - de Jong & Perfetti (2011), *Language Learning* 61(2): fluency gained by repeating the same speech carried over to a new topic, which they read as proceduralisation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00620.x)
- [EXP] [unverified] **Planning time.** Yuan & Ellis (2003), *Applied Linguistics* 24(1): **pre-task planning** increased the fluency and complexity of oral narratives; unpressured online planning increased accuracy and complexity. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/24.1.1)

**B. Classroom-discourse evidence and frameworks (Walsh, IRF, scaffolding, negotiation)**
- [Framework + CA studies] **Walsh's classroom interactional competence (CIC).** Its self-evaluation (SETT) instrument lists 14 interactional features:
  - scaffolding, direct repair, content feedback, extended wait time, referential questions, seeking clarification, confirmation checks, extended learner turn
  - teacher echo, teacher interruptions, extended teacher turn, turn completion, display questions, form-focused feedback
  - Sources: operationalised in [ERIC EJ1408193 (Teachers' CIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1408193.pdf) and [ERIC EJ1258546](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1258546.pdf); Walsh, [*Conceptualising classroom interactional competence*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286696054_Conceptualising_classroom_interactional_competence).
- **Interactional space.** Teachers widen it by allowing **more wait time and planning time** and encouraging **extended learner turns**. Less **evaluative closure** plus more wait time raises participation. Sources as above.
- **Walsh's three scaffolding moves:** **reformulation** (rephrasing the learner's contribution), **extension** (building on it) and **modelling** (giving an example). Sources as above.
- **Supportive vs hindering features.** Small observational studies using Walsh's framework found that seeking clarification, content feedback, scaffolding and extended wait time support learning, while **teacher echo and display questions hinder** it.
  - Sources: [ERIC EJ1408193](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1408193.pdf); [review study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/399672417_The_Role_of_Teacher_Talk_and_Interactional_Scaffolding_in_Modern_Communicative_Classrooms_for_ESL_A_Review_Study); [Semarang classroom study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357874081_Walsh's_Classroom_Modes_and_Interactional_Features_of_Teacher_Talk_in_Science_Class_at_DCS_Semarang).
- [CA, 2002] [unverified] **Construction vs obstruction.** Walsh (2002), *Language Teaching Research* 6(1).
  - Features that **construct** learner involvement: direct error correction, content feedback, checking for confirmation, extended wait time, scaffolding.
  - Features that **obstruct** it: turn completion (finishing learners' sentences), teacher echo, teacher interruptions.
  - [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1191/1362168802lr095oa)
- [unverified] **Definition of CIC.** Walsh (2011/2012): "teachers' and learners' ability to use interaction as a tool for mediating and assisting learning". [Walsh 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286696054_Conceptualising_classroom_interactional_competence)
- [CA, 2008] **Explicit praise.** Waring (2008), *Modern Language Journal*: in the third turn, explicit positive assessments ("Very good", "Excellent") tend to **suppress opportunities for learners to voice problems of understanding or to explore other correct answers**. In one case, a learner's doubt only surfaced many lines after the topic had been closed. [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2008.00788.x)
- [CA, 2009] **Leaving IRF.** Waring (2009), *Language Learning*, single-case analysis of **moving out of IRF**. A related paper documents **student initiatives and missed learning opportunities** inside IRF sequences.
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2009.00526.x); [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9tf451nb).
- [CA] [unverified] **The third move.** Nassaji & Wells (2000), *Applied Linguistics* 21(3): IRF is not bad in itself. A third move that **asks for justification, elaboration or connections** opens up dialogue; an evaluative third move tends to close it. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/21.3.376)
- [CA] [unverified] **Kinds of follow-up.** Cullen (2002), *ELT Journal* 56(2), separates **evaluative** from **discoursal** follow-up moves. Discoursal follow-up (reformulating, elaborating, commenting, repeating) builds learners' ideas into the ongoing talk. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/56.2.117)
- [unverified] **Scaffolding functions.** Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) list six: recruitment, **reduction in degrees of freedom**, direction maintenance, marking critical features, **frustration control**, demonstration. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x)
- [REVIEW] [unverified] **Core of scaffolding.** van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen (2010): **contingency** (fit to the learner's current level), **fading**, and **transfer of responsibility**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6)
- [unverified] **Interaction Hypothesis.** Long (1996): "negotiation for meaning, and especially negotiation work that triggers interactional adjustments by the NS or more competent interlocutor, facilitates acquisition because it connects input, internal learner capacities, particularly selective attention, and output in productive ways."
  - Sources: [WorldCat, *Handbook of Second Language Acquisition*, 1996](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Handbook+of+second+language+acquisition+Ritchie+Bhatia+1996); review in [Loewen & Sato (2018)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/interaction-and-instructed-second-language-acquisition/78A156EE200F744F5978F99BFB073DBE).
- [unverified] **Negotiation moves.** Pica (1994), *Language Learning* 44(3): **clarification requests, confirmation checks and comprehension checks** push learners to modify their output. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1994.tb01115.x)
- [unverified] **Four strands.** Nation (2007): a balanced course gives about equal time to meaning-focused input, **meaning-focused output**, language-focused learning and **fluency development**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2167/illt039.0)

**C. Practitioner consensus**
- [PRACT/action research, 1996] [unverified] **Thornbury (1996), *ELT Journal* 50(4).** Teachers who analysed transcripts of their own lessons found display questions and evaluative feedback dominating. Thornbury proposed four markers of "communicative" teacher talk: **referential questions, content (not form) feedback, wait time, and student-initiated talk**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/50.4.279)
- [PRACT] **Quality over quantity.** The CIC literature does not aim simply to minimise teacher talk. Teacher talk should fit the teaching goal of the moment and **create interactional space**. [Walsh 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286696054_Conceptualising_classroom_interactional_competence); [ERIC EJ1408193](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1408193.pdf)

### Inferences
These are design inferences drawn from the evidence above; no study tested them in an AI tutor.

- **Turn shape.** Keep each tutor turn short. React to the *content* of what the learner said in one sentence, then ask **one** open, personal question.
  - Never stack several questions in one turn.
  - Track **learner speaking share** (target ≥60–70% of speech time), **mean length of learner turn**, and **learner-initiated turns** as live metrics.
  - These targets are product choices, not empirically derived optima.
- **Question ladder for "understand but can't speak" learners.** Start with referential questions about the learner's own life (Brock). If the learner is silent after about 3–5 s, **reduce the degrees of freedom** rather than answering for them:
  1. Open question: "What did you do at the weekend?"
  2. Either/or: "Did you stay home or go out?"
  3. Sentence starter: "I went to…"
  4. Once they answer, fade back up: "Why?" or "Tell me more."
- **Wait time in a voice interface.**
  - Wait ≥3 s before rephrasing a question (Rowe).
  - After the learner pauses, wait about 2 s before taking the turn, because >2 s pauses shifted talk away from IRF (Smith & King).
  - Use **backchannels** ("mm-hm", "right…", "go on") to hold the floor open instead of taking it.
  - Set end-of-turn detection to tolerate long mid-utterance pauses. L2 speakers hesitate more, so native-speaker defaults will cut them off. This is an engineering assumption; no source was found.
  - Say it explicitly: "Take your time, I'll wait."
- **Replace IRF-with-praise with uptake plus a new question.** LLM tutors naturally slide into "Great job! Next question…".
  - Instead, make the third turn discoursal (Cullen; Nassaji & Wells; Waring): take up the content and extend it.
  - Example: Learner: "Yesterday I go to cinema." → Tutor: "Oh, you went to the cinema yesterday! What did you see?" This embeds the recast in a genuine reply.
  - Save explicit praise for the end of a task and make it specific.
- **Forbidden behaviours** (Walsh 2002): echoing the learner verbatim, finishing their sentences, interrupting, long tutor monologues, and quiz-style display questions during conversation.
- **Negotiation of meaning.** A strong speech model understands almost everything, so real negotiation will be rare.
  - When an utterance is genuinely ambiguous or unfinished, use a real **clarification request or confirmation check** ("Do you mean you changed jobs or changed teams?") instead of silently guessing. This pushes modified output (Long; Pica).
  - Never fake misunderstanding; it frustrates learners.
- **Error policy.**
  - **During fluency tasks:** at most one salient error per learner turn, handled by an implicit recast inside a content reply.
  - **In a short accuracy slot:** use **prompts** that trigger self-repair ("Yesterday I…?"), since prompts beat recasts (Lyster & Saito).
  - **For high-anxiety learners:** move explicit correction into a **private post-task summary**, because recasts may not help them (Sheen).
- **Build repetition and planning into every lesson.** Give 20–60 s of think time with key words before a long turn. Then have the learner do the same task **2–3 times** (4/3/2 or "tell it again, a bit faster, add one detail"). This builds fluency and also produces a measurable before/after for the progress screen (see §4).
- **Balance the 20 minutes** roughly along Nation's four strands: some input (the tutor's model answer), mostly meaning-focused output, a short language focus drawn from the learner's own utterances, and a fluency repetition.

### Gaps
- No verified modern figure was found for the typical teacher-talk share in EFL lessons. No study was found that sets an optimal teacher-to-student talk ratio for one-to-one or AI tutoring.
- Exact effect sizes (d) from Mackey & Goo (2007) and Lyster & Saito (2010) could not be retrieved.
  - One search summary gave d = 1.09 (pre–post change averaged over lexis and grammar) for Mackey & Goo, apparently via Vos et al. (2018), [*Language Learning*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12296). This is unconfirmed.
- Wait-time evidence comes from L1 science classes (Rowe) and Japanese university EFL (Smith & King). No evidence was found on optimal silence or end-of-turn thresholds for L2 speakers in voice-AI systems.
- Evidence on the Long & Sato (1983) finding that display questions dominate ESL classrooms was not retrieved.
- All [unverified] items above need checking before exact figures or quotations are used.

## 2. Personalisation, relevance and motivation (Dörnyei's motivational strategies, the L2 Motivational Self System, goal-setting, autonomy)

### Takeaway
Teachers' motivational practice is linked to learners' motivated behaviour. A vivid **ideal L2 self** strongly predicts **intended effort** (r = .61) but only weakly predicts **measured achievement** (r = .20). Vision and personal relevance are therefore mainly engagement and retention levers. They must be paired with **concrete goals** and real speaking practice: in the one intervention found, visualisation alone was not enough to raise willingness to communicate (WTC).

### Cited Findings

**A. Quantitative evidence**
- [META] **L2 Motivational Self System.** Al-Hoorie (2018), *SSLLT* 8(4): 721–754, the first meta-analysis of the system: 32 reports, 39 samples, **32,078 learners**.

  | Component | r with intended effort | r with objective achievement |
  |---|---|---|
  | Ideal L2 self | .61 | .20 |
  | Ought-to L2 self | .38 | −.05 |
  | L2 learning experience | .41 | .17 |

  - Heterogeneity was substantial.
  - The link between L2 learning experience and effort is partly inflated because the two scales share wording.
  - Sources: [SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/12295); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1202469).
- [CORR, classroom observation, 2008] **Motivational teaching in practice.** Guilloteaux & Dörnyei (2008), *TESOL Quarterly* 42(1): 55–77.
  - Setting: 40 ESOL classrooms in South Korea, 27 teachers, more than 1,300 learners.
  - Teachers' motivational practice was measured by classroom observation plus the observer's post-hoc rating, and correlated with learners' observed motivated behaviour and self-reported motivation.
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2008.tb00207.x); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ818252).
  - [unverified] Reported result, from the abstract as recalled: teachers' motivational practice was significantly linked to learners' motivated behaviour and motivational state.
- [QEXP] [unverified] **Trained motivational strategies.** Moskovsky, Alrabai, Paolini & Ratcheva (2013), *Language Learning* 63(1), Saudi EFL. Teachers trained in 10 motivational strategies used them for about 8 weeks. Learners' self-reported motivation rose significantly more than in control classes. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2012.00717.x)
- [QEXP] **Visualisation and goal-setting.** Munezane (2015), *Modern Language Journal* 99(1): 175–191, with **373 Japanese university EFL learners** in three groups:
  - control;
  - **visualisation** (imagining themselves as future specialists discussing global issues);
  - **visualisation + goal-setting** (stating their speaking goals each class).
  - Amounts of communicative activity were similar across groups.
  - It was the first intervention to use ideal-L2-self visualisation and goal-setting to raise L2 WTC.
  - Design verified: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12193); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1059529).
  - [unverified] Result, from the abstract as recalled: only **visualisation + goal-setting** raised WTC significantly over control; visualisation alone did not.
  - Related: [Munezane, EUROSLA Yearbook 16](https://benjamins.com/catalog/eurosla.16.04mun), on ideal self and WTC as predictors of observed classroom L2 use (content not accessed).
- [QEXP, small] [unverified] **Vision programmes.** Magid & Chan (2012), *Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching* 6(2). Programmes in the UK and Hong Kong combined imagery, goal-setting and action plans; participants reported a stronger ideal L2 self and more confidence. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17501229.2011.614693)
- [LONG] [unverified] **Goal-setting and achievement.** Moeller, Theiler & Wu (2012), *Modern Language Journal* 96(2). A 5-year study of US high-school Spanish learners who set goals with the LinguaFolio can-do portfolio found a **significant positive relationship between goal-setting and language achievement**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2011.01231.x)
- [REVIEW] [unverified] **Goal-setting theory.** Locke & Latham (2002), *American Psychologist* 57(9): **specific, challenging goals** outperform vague "do your best" goals, and goals work best **with feedback on progress**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)
- [CORR] [unverified] **Autonomy and self-determination.**
  - Noels, Clément & Pelletier (1999), *Modern Language Journal* 83(1): learners who saw their teacher as **controlling** and giving little **informative feedback** reported lower intrinsic motivation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/0026-7902.00003)
  - Noels et al. (2000), *Language Learning* 50(1): more self-determined reasons for learning went with higher perceived competence, lower anxiety and a stronger intention to continue. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00111)
- [META] [unverified] **Self-reference effect.** Symons & Johnson (1997), *Psychological Bulletin* 121(3): information processed in relation to oneself is remembered better. This is the cognitive rationale for personalising content. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.3.371)

**B. Frameworks and practitioner consensus**
- **The L2 Motivational Self System** has three parts: ideal L2 self, ought-to L2 self and L2 learning experience. [Al-Hoorie 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/12295)
- [CORR/PRACT, 1998] [unverified] **"Ten commandments".** Dörnyei & Csizér (1998), *Language Teaching Research* 2(3), surveyed 200 Hungarian teachers who rated 51 strategies. The top ten:
  1. Set a personal example with your own behaviour.
  2. Create a pleasant, relaxed atmosphere.
  3. Present tasks properly.
  4. Develop a good relationship with learners.
  5. Increase learners' linguistic self-confidence.
  6. Make classes interesting.
  7. Promote learner autonomy.
  8. **Personalise the learning process.**
  9. Increase learners' goal-orientedness.
  10. Familiarise learners with the target-language culture.

  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/136216889800200302)
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Four-phase framework.** Dörnyei (2001), *Motivational Strategies in the Language Classroom*:
  1. create basic motivational conditions;
  2. generate initial motivation;
  3. maintain and protect motivation;
  4. encourage **positive retrospective self-evaluation**: attributing results to effort, motivational feedback, rewards.

  [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Motivational+strategies+in+the+language+classroom+Dornyei)
- [PRACT/theory] [unverified] **When a future self-image motivates.** Dörnyei (2009) and Dörnyei & Kubanyiova (2014): it works only if it is
  - **vivid and elaborate**;
  - **plausible**;
  - not in conflict with social expectations;
  - **regularly activated**;
  - backed by a **concrete roadmap** of steps;
  - balanced by a feared self.

  [WorldCat, *Motivating Learners, Motivating Teachers*](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Motivating+learners+motivating+teachers+building+vision+in+the+language+classroom)

### Inferences
- **The onboarding interview is both a needs analysis and a vision-building step.** Elicit:
  - why the learner needs English;
  - 2–3 concrete scenarios (work calls, interviews, travel, relocation, a child's school);
  - what they are afraid of;
  - a vivid "future me speaking English" image.

  Store these and refer back to them in every lesson. This keeps the vision **regularly activated** (Dörnyei).
- **Never offer vision without a goal.** Every lesson opens with one concrete speaking goal tied to that scenario ("Today: introduce yourself and your job on a video call") and closes by checking it (Munezane, Locke & Latham).
- **Personalise the conversation content itself.** Use the learner's job, family, city and hobbies as the topics, and recycle phrases they produced earlier (Dörnyei & Csizér; self-reference effect).
- **Be autonomy-supportive** (Noels):
  - offer choices (topic A or B; "more talking" vs "more correction"; pace);
  - give reasons ("We'll repeat this story so it gets easier");
  - avoid controlling language ("you must", "wrong").
- **Use the effort–achievement split commercially.** The ideal L2 self predicts effort (r = .61), which helps retention and willingness to pay. Achievement, which drives long-term renewal, needs actual speaking volume and feedback.
- **Positive retrospective self-evaluation at the end of each lesson.** Attribute success to strategy and effort, not talent. Example: "You kept going for 3 minutes. Using 'Let me think…' really helped."

### Gaps
- No study was found on motivation or ideal-L2-self interventions **specifically for adult Russian-speaking learners** or for **AI-delivered** vision building.
- Effect sizes for Guilloteaux & Dörnyei (2008) and Munezane (2015) could not be retrieved in this session.
- No meta-analysis of motivational-strategy **interventions** in L2 was located.

## 3. Foreign language (speaking) anxiety, willingness to communicate and enjoyment, and what reduces anxiety

### Takeaway
- **Anxiety** correlates moderately and negatively with achievement (r = −.36 across 105 samples) and with WTC (r ≈ −.38).
- **Enjoyment** correlates positively with achievement (r = .30) and more strongly with WTC (r = .48 to .64).
- **Anxiety can change:** intervention meta-analyses show medium effects.
- **Teacher behaviour moves enjoyment more than anxiety:** the teacher's use of the language, humour and time given to speaking raise enjoyment, while anxiety is more learner-internal.

### Cited Findings

**A. Meta-analytic evidence**
- [META] **Anxiety and achievement.** Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky (2019), *SSLA* 41(2): 363–387.
  - 97 reports, 105 samples, **N = 19,933**, 23 countries, 216 effect sizes.
  - **Mean r = −.36** between L2 anxiety and achievement.
  - Effects varied by type of achievement measure, educational level, target language and type of anxiety. They were strongest at lower educational levels (elementary).
  - Sources: [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330182451_SECOND_LANGUAGE_ANXIETY_AND_ACHIEVEMENT_A_META-ANALYSIS); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1221543); [erratum](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/second-language-anxiety-and-achievement-a-metaanalysis-erratum/1726B408D549071AC135A1FE53AFA918).
- [META] [unverified] **The anxiety scale and achievement.** Botes, Dewaele & Greiff (2020), *Journal for the Psychology of Language Learning* 2(1): FLCAS scores and academic achievement, pooled r ≈ −.39. [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1352561.pdf)

  - Sources: [SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/34235); [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1354288.pdf).
- [META, 2026] **Emotions and WTC.** Man & Gu (2026), *Educational Research Review* 52: 100806, 64 studies from the past 15 years.
  - Enjoyment–WTC **r = .636** (large).

  - Anxiety–WTC r = −.376.
  - Moderators tested: culture, educational level, target language, instrument.
  - [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X2600045X)
- [META] **Correlates of WTC.** Elahi Shirvan, Khajavy, MacIntyre & Taherian (2019), *Journal of Psycholinguistic Research* 48(6): 1241–1267. **Perceived communicative competence, language anxiety and motivation** are the three "high-evidence" correlates of L2 WTC, and all three correlate moderately with it. Exact correlations were not retrieved. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-019-09656-9)
- [META, 2024] **Anxiety interventions.** Xiong et al. (2024), *Journal of Language and Social Psychology* 43(5–6): 620–650, 37 intervention studies.
  - Interventions reduced foreign language anxiety (**ES = −0.61**, p = .03).
  - Of seven moderators, **only age** was significant.
  - Anxiety-targeted and teaching-based interventions were about equally common.
  - [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X241291258)
- [META] **Speaking-anxiety interventions.** A meta-analysis on ijopr.com (ERIC EJ1478455) reports a **medium** overall effect; intervention type and duration were not significant moderators. Authors and exact figures were not retrieved. [IJOPR](https://www.ijopr.com/article/the-effect-of-intervention-studies-on-foreign-language-speaking-anxiety-a-meta-analysis-study-16361); [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1478455.pdf)
- [META, venue of unclear standing] **Another speaking-anxiety meta-analysis.** "Alleviating foreign language speaking anxiety: a meta-analysis of pedagogical interventions", *International Journal of Pedagogics*:
  - 62 studies, 4,318 learners, **g = 0.85** [0.71, 0.99];
  - **technology-based interventions had larger effects** than traditional ones;
  - longer interventions gave larger reductions.

**B. Individual studies on teacher behaviour, enjoyment and anxiety**
- [CORR] **Teacher and learner variables.** Dewaele, Witney, Saito & Dewaele (2018), *Language Teaching Research* 22(6): 676–697, with 189 British secondary-school language learners.
  - **Higher enjoyment** went with positive attitudes to the language and **to the teacher**, the **teacher's use of the language in class**, the **proportion of class time spent speaking**, and the learner's relative standing and stage.
  - **Lower anxiety** went mainly with learner-internal variables: attitude to the language, relative standing, stage.
  - Sources: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168817692161); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1194097).
- [LONG] **Teacher behaviour over a semester.** Dewaele, Saito & Halimi (*Language Teaching Research*, online 2022): 360 university learners of English, German, French and Spanish in Kuwait.
  - The teacher's **frequency of language use, predictability and frequency of joking** were positively related to **enjoyment** and to attitude/motivation, but **not to anxiety**.
  - Enjoyment **dropped among students whose teacher rarely joked**.
  - [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13621688221089601)
  - The direction of the "predictability" effect should be checked in the full text.
- [CORR] [unverified] **Enjoyment and anxiety are separate.** Dewaele & MacIntyre (2014), *SSLLT* 4(2), with 1,746 learners worldwide: the two are moderately negatively correlated but are **separate dimensions, not two ends of one scale**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2014.4.2.5)
- [unverified] **Teacher-specificity.** Dewaele & Dewaele (2020), *SSLLT*, asked whether enjoyment and anxiety are specific to the teacher. Recalled finding: enjoyment varied more with the teacher than anxiety did. [SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/22349)

**C. Construct and mechanisms (classic work)**
- [1986] **Definition.** Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope (1986), *Modern Language Journal* 70(2).
  - Foreign language anxiety is a **distinct complex of self-perceptions, beliefs, feelings and behaviours** tied to classroom language learning and arising from the uniqueness of that process.
  - It is related to **communication apprehension, test anxiety and fear of negative evaluation**, but cannot be reduced to their sum.
  - The FLCAS has **33 items**; Horwitz (1986) reported test–retest reliability of .83.
  - Sources: [Horwitz et al. PDF](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/horowitz.pdf); [review](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1078777.pdf); [PMC overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9377287/); [*Annual Review of Applied Linguistics* review of construct, effects and sources](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/second-language-anxiety-construct-effects-and-sources/2FFCB23F8EA271606AED54CC7088358D).
- [1986] [unverified] **The "understand but can't speak" profile.** Horwitz et al. described anxious learners who **freeze** in oral activities and say they **know a grammar point but "forget" it when speaking**. [PDF](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/horowitz.pdf)
- [EXP] [unverified] **Cognitive effects.** MacIntyre & Gardner (1994), *Language Learning* 44(2): anxiety disrupts the **input, processing and output** stages. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1994.tb01103.x)
- [Theory] [unverified] **Willingness to communicate.** MacIntyre, Clément, Dörnyei & Noels (1998), *Modern Language Journal* 82(4).
  - WTC is "a readiness to enter into discourse at a particular time with a specific person or persons, using a L2".
  - They present it as a heuristic pyramid. Its immediate antecedents are the **desire to communicate with a specific person** and **state communicative self-confidence** (perceived competence plus low anxiety).
  - [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1998.tb05543.x)
- [REVIEW, 1991] [unverified] **Sources of anxiety.** Young (1991), *Modern Language Journal* 75(4), names six:
  - personal and interpersonal anxieties;
  - learner beliefs;
  - instructor beliefs;
  - instructor–learner interaction (e.g., **harsh error correction**);
  - classroom procedures (e.g., **speaking in front of the group**);
  - testing.

  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1991.tb05378.x)
- [Qualitative, small] [unverified] **Perfectionism.** Gregersen & Horwitz (2002), *Modern Language Journal* 86(4): anxious learners watching their own oral performance showed **perfectionist** tendencies: very high standards, concern over errors, fear of evaluation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-4781.00161)
- [QEXP] [unverified] **Communication-strategy training.** Dörnyei (1995), *TESOL Quarterly* 29(1): six weeks of training in fillers and hesitation devices, circumlocution, and topic avoidance or replacement improved strategy use and speech rate. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/3587805)
- **Short validated scales for measuring these constructs in-app:**
  - short-form FLCAS: [*Applied Linguistics*, 2022](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/43/5/1006/6571635);
  - short-form Foreign Language Enjoyment Scale: [*Modern Language Journal*, 2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12741);
  - [unverified] item counts: about 8 and about 9 items respectively.

### Inferences
- **Remove the audience.** Fear of negative evaluation is a core component of anxiety (Horwitz). Remove every evaluative signal during speaking:
  - no scores or red marks mid-conversation;
  - no correction that interrupts the learner;
  - frame openly: "Mistakes are normal here; I'm listening for your ideas."

  The private one-to-one setting is a selling point (see §6).
- **Engineer perceived competence early.** Perceived competence is one of the strongest correlates of WTC. The first 2–3 minutes of every lesson, and especially the first lesson, should be **easy, successful talk** using language the learner already has, such as their job, family or city. Only then should the task be stretched.
- **Teach "anti-barrier" communication strategies explicitly in lesson 1** (Dörnyei 1995):
  - time-gainers: "Let me think…", "How can I say…";
  - circumlocution: "It's a kind of…", "the thing you use for…";
  - asking for help: "What's the word for…?"

  These give "freezing" learners a way to keep talking.
- **Allow Russian as a lifeline, not as the channel.** If the learner is stuck, let them say the word in Russian and the tutor supplies the English, then continue in English. Maximising target-language use is associated with enjoyment (Dewaele). The Russian lifeline itself is untested here and remains a practitioner inference.
- **Design for enjoyment, not only for low anxiety.** Enjoyment relates more strongly to WTC (r = .48–.64) than anxiety does (r ≈ −.38), and teacher behaviour moves enjoyment more than anxiety. The tutor persona should be:
  - warm and curious about the learner's life;
  - lightly humorous;
  - varied and a little unpredictable in topics and tasks;
  - consistent in maximising English.
- **Reframe perfectionism** (Gregersen & Horwitz). Define success as "was understood" and "kept talking", not "was error-free". Show that fillers and self-corrections are normal even in native speech.
- **Correct gently and later.** Harsh or public correction is a named source of anxiety (Young). Anxious learners also benefit less from recasts (Sheen). Default to a delayed private summary of 2–3 "upgrades", with an optional "correct me more" setting.
- **Measure it.** Give the short FLCAS and short enjoyment scale at onboarding and every few weeks for product analytics and A/B tests. Correlations suggest a 1-point drop in anxiety matters.

### Gaps
- No studies were retrieved on foreign language anxiety in **Russian-speaking adult** learners specifically, or on the culturally discussed "языковой барьер" (language barrier). Whether Russian schooling's emphasis on accuracy feeds perfectionism is a **hypothesis**, not a sourced finding.
- The exact WTC correlations in Elahi Shirvan et al. (2019) were not retrieved.
- Intervention meta-analyses conflict on whether technology-based or longer interventions work better; the result favouring them comes from a low-credibility venue.
- Several relevant recent papers were located but not accessed:
  - [2026 three-level meta-analysis on enjoyment](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17501229.2026.2699419);
  - [anxiety, enjoyment and boredom vs achievement (*Annual Review of Applied Linguistics*)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/threebody-problem-the-effects-of-foreign-language-anxiety-enjoyment-and-boredom-on-academic-achievement/40F0D8E676AB3EEDF5B27120D15DC354);
  - [causal roles of motivation, enjoyment and anxiety in L2 speech learning (*SSLA*)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/disentangling-the-causal-role-of-motivation-enjoyment-and-anxiety-in-second-language-speech-learning-a-final-report/21FA00EBF590AD26A9B2F6449FEFFB04);
  - [boredom and achievement meta-analysis (*SSLA*)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/relationship-between-boredom-and-second-language-achievement-a-multilevel-metaanalysis/8DBF552BDE74CC6BF1DF9ED4BB68895D).

## 4. Self-efficacy and showing progress: feedback on progress, can-do statements, goal gradient, streaks, gamification and habit formation (brief)

### Takeaway
L2 self-efficacy is one of the strongest belief-level correlates of achievement (subgroup r ≈ .47–.62). By classic theory it is built mainly through **mastery experiences**, plus **specific progress feedback against a clear goal**. Showing a head start and how close the learner is to a milestone increases persistence. Streaks and gamification have small-to-medium effects, mostly on engagement rather than learning.

### Cited Findings
- [META] **Self-efficacy and achievement.** Goetze & Driver (2022), *SSLLT* 12(2): 233–259. Subgroup correlations:

  | Subgroup | r |
  |---|---|
  | GPA | .62 |
  | Course grades | .47 |
  | Language tests | .47 |
  | English as target language | .53 |
  | Other languages | .47 |
  | Institutional contexts | .47–.50 |

  - The authors present self-efficacy as among the strongest predictors of L2 achievement. The overall pooled r was not retrieved.
  - Sources: [SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/34236); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361455259_Is_learning_really_just_believing_A_meta-analysis_of_self-efficacy_and_achievement_in_SLA).
- [Theory, 1977] [unverified] **Sources of self-efficacy.** Bandura (1977) names four:
  - **mastery (performance) experiences**, the most influential;
  - vicarious experience;
  - verbal persuasion;
  - physiological and emotional states.

  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.2.191)
- [EXP] [unverified] **Goals plus progress feedback.** Schunk & Swartz (1993), *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 18(3): children given a **process goal plus regular progress feedback** had the highest self-efficacy, writing skill and strategy transfer. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1993.1024)
- [REVIEW] [unverified] **What good feedback does.** Hattie & Timperley (2007): it answers **"Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next?"** Feedback about the task, the process and self-regulation works; **praise aimed at the person is least effective**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487)
- [META] [unverified] **Feedback effect sizes.**
  - Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), 435 studies: mean feedback effect d = 0.48; high-information feedback is most effective. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087)
  - Kluger & DeNisi (1996): mean d = 0.41, but **more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance**, especially when feedback drew attention to the self. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254)
- [Frameworks] [unverified] **Can-do descriptors.**
  - The **CEFR Companion Volume** (Council of Europe, 2020) provides illustrative "can do" descriptors for spoken production, interaction and mediation. [Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages)
  - The **NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements** (2017). [ACTFL](https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/ncssfl-actfl-can-do-statements)
  - Goal-setting with a can-do portfolio was linked to achievement (Moeller et al. 2012, see §2).
- [Field experiment] [unverified] **Endowed progress.** Nunes & Drèze (2006), *Journal of Consumer Research* 32(4). Two car-wash loyalty cards both needed 8 purchases: one had 8 empty slots, the other 10 slots with 2 pre-stamped. **Completion was 19% vs 34%**, and the head-start group finished faster. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480)
- [Field experiment] [unverified] **Goal gradient.** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006), *Journal of Marketing Research* 43(1): customers **bought more often as they neared the reward**, and a 12-stamp card with 2 pre-filled stamps was completed faster than a 10-stamp card ("illusionary goal progress"). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)
- [Diary study] [unverified] **Small wins.** Amabile & Kramer (2011): in about 12,000 diary entries from 238 knowledge workers, **making progress in meaningful work** was the biggest booster of motivation and positive emotion. [HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)
- [Field study] [unverified] **Habit formation.** Lally et al. (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6): a new daily behaviour took a **median of 66 days (range 18–254)** to reach 95% of its automaticity plateau, and missing a single day did not materially impair habit formation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- [META] [unverified] **Implementation intentions.** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006): "if situation X, then I do Y" plans improved goal attainment, d = 0.65 across 94 tests. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)
- [META] [unverified] **Gamification.** Sailer & Homner (2020), *Educational Psychology Review* 32: effects on learning were cognitive g = 0.49, motivational g = 0.36, behavioural g = 0.25. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- [unverified] **Duolingo-specific studies.**
  - Loewen et al. (2019), *ReCALL* 31(3), a small case study: study time was related to gains, and motivation varied. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344019000065)
  - Shortt et al. (2023), *CALL* 36(3), a systematic review of Duolingo research 2012–2020. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2021.1933540)
- Related items located but not accessed:
  - [mobile-app learners' self-efficacy study](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/b563/9875d91285bf3abc5cdff3e3102415203d9f.pdf);
  - [meta-analysis of self-regulated-learning interventions on L2 achievement and self-efficacy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9650592/);
  - [Leeming (2025), *TESOL Quarterly*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tesq.3412), whose title reports self-efficacy and collective efficacy as predictors of engagement in group conversation.

### Inferences
- **The first lesson must create a real mastery experience plus a visible before/after.** It converts only if the learner leaves thinking "I spoke English for real, more than I thought I could." Recommended mechanics:
  1. The learner tells the same short personal story 2–3 times (§1).
  2. The app shows **objective within-lesson deltas**: total minutes spoken, longest uninterrupted turn, words per minute or pause count on attempt 1 vs attempt 3, and the number of new phrases actually used.
  3. It closes with a **can-do statement**: "Now you can introduce your job and say why you like it (≈A2+)".
  4. It ends on **"where to next"**.
- **Feedback format:** "What you did well (specific) → one thing to upgrade → next goal" (Hattie & Timperley). Avoid person-level praise ("You're so talented"). Kluger & DeNisi show this kind of feedback can backfire.
- **Endowed progress and goal gradient in the course map.**
  - Count onboarding and lesson 1 as the first steps toward a visible first milestone, such as "Level: Small talk at work, 2/10 done".
  - Show "1 lesson to your next skill badge" as the learner approaches.

  These are marketing-lab effects, not tested in language apps; A/B test them.
- **Streaks, carefully.**
  - Offer streak protection (freezes).
  - Never shame a broken streak; Lally found one missed day doesn't break habit formation.
  - Ask for an implementation intention at onboarding ("When and where will you do your 20 minutes?").
  - Expect the first ~2 months to be the fragile habit-formation window, and plan retention nudges for it.
- **Keep gamification in its lane.** Its effects are small-to-medium and mainly behavioural. Use it for habit, not as a substitute for speaking volume.
- **Keep progress metrics honest and robust.** Metrics such as words per minute can drop when a learner attempts more complex language. Pair fluency metrics with complexity and "new phrase used" metrics so learners are not punished for trying.

### Gaps
- **No verified Duolingo data** was retrieved on streaks, streak freezes, leaderboards, retention or learning outcomes. Such figures appear in company blogs and press but could not be checked, so do not quote them without verification.
- No study was found on the validity of **learner self-assessment with can-do statements for speaking in adult app learners**, nor on endowed-progress or goal-gradient effects **inside language apps**.
- The overall pooled correlation in Goetze & Driver (2022) was not retrieved.

## 5. Practitioner consensus (Thornbury, Harmer, Scrivener, Underhill, Walsh) on rapport and speaking activities, and what distinguishes expert teachers' interaction

### Takeaway
Leading teacher trainers converge on a few points:
- **Teacher talk:** less, but better, with elicitation instead of explanation.
- **Rapport:** built through genuine interest, listening, respect and remembering the learner.
- **Stretching learners:** push them gently past "good enough" (Demand High).
- **Emergent language:** work with the language learners produce themselves (Dogme).
- **Repetition:** repeat and recycle speaking tasks.

This consensus is only partly tested. The best-supported pieces are that teacher behaviours (use of the language, humour, time spent speaking) predict enjoyment, and that some interactional features lengthen learner turns (§1, §3).

### Cited Findings
Items in this section are practitioner consensus (books) and were not re-checked in this session unless noted.
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Harmer** (*How to Teach English*, 2nd ed. 2007; *The Practice of English Language Teaching*, 5th ed. 2015). Rapport rests on four things:
  - **recognising students** (names, remembering personal details);
  - **listening** to them;
  - **respecting** them;
  - **being even-handed**.

  His Engage–Study–Activate sequence reserves "Activate" for free speaking. [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Harmer+The+practice+of+English+language+teaching)
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Scrivener** (*Learning Teaching*, 3rd ed. 2011) grounds rapport in Carl Rogers' core conditions: **respect** (unconditional positive regard), **empathy** and **authenticity** (genuineness). He also advocates cutting unnecessary teacher talk in favour of elicitation. [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Scrivener+Learning+Teaching+2011)
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Scrivener & Underhill, "Demand High" ELT** (from 2012). Stop "going through the motions" and unlock each activity's learning potential by **asking for more**: say it again better, extend it, reformulate it. Do not accept the first adequate answer. [Demand High ELT](https://demandhighelt.wordpress.com/)
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Underhill (1999)**, in Arnold (ed.), *Affect in Language Learning*: teacher roles run from lecturer to teacher to **facilitator**. The facilitator actively manages the psychological climate and the learner's inner process, not just the content. [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Affect+in+language+learning+Arnold+1999)
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Thornbury (2005)**, *How to Teach Speaking*:
  - speaking develops through **awareness → appropriation → autonomy**;
  - fluency rests on automatised chunks;
  - he recommends rehearsal, repetition and real communicative tasks.

  [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Thornbury+How+to+teach+speaking)
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Meddings & Thornbury (2009)**, *Teaching Unplugged* (Dogme ELT): conversation-driven, materials-light teaching with a **focus on emergent language**. The teacher scaffolds, reformulates and upgrades the learner's own utterances and recycles them. [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Teaching+unplugged+Meddings+Thornbury)
- [PRACT] [unverified] **Goh & Burns (2012)**, *Teaching Speaking: A Holistic Approach*, seven-stage teaching-speaking cycle:
  1. focus attention on speaking;
  2. provide input and guide planning;
  3. conduct the speaking task;
  4. focus on language, skills and strategies;
  5. **repeat the speaking task**;
  6. direct learners' reflection;
  7. facilitate feedback.

  [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Goh+Burns+Teaching+speaking+a+holistic+approach)
- [PRACT, 1996] [unverified] **Thornbury (1996)**, markers of communicative teacher talk: referential questions, content feedback, wait time, student-initiated talk. See §1. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/50.4.279)
- [Case studies] [unverified] **Tsui (2003)**, *Understanding Expertise in Teaching*: expert ESL teachers respond **more flexibly and contingently** to learners, integrate their knowledge domains, and keep problematising their own practice. [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/search?q=Tsui+Understanding+expertise+in+teaching)
- [Framework] **Walsh's classroom interactional competence** (see §1) is the most research-based description of expert teacher interaction. It covers shaping learner contributions through scaffolding, clarification and content feedback, and creating space through wait time and extended learner turns. [ERIC EJ1408193](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1408193.pdf); [Walsh 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286696054_Conceptualising_classroom_interactional_competence)
- [Empirical support for parts of the consensus] Teacher-linked variables predict enjoyment but much less so anxiety: the teacher's use of the language, time spent speaking, attitude to the teacher, and joking (Dewaele et al. 2018; Dewaele, Saito & Halimi 2022).
  - Sources: [SAGE 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168817692161); [SAGE 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13621688221089601).
- [META, general education] [unverified] **Teacher–student relationships.** Cornelius-White (2007), *Review of Educational Research* 77(1), 119 studies: learner-centred relationships (empathy, warmth, genuineness, encouraging thinking) correlate positively with cognitive and affective outcomes. The commonly cited mean is r ≈ .3; verify. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298563)

### Inferences
Tutor persona and behaviour rules translated from the consensus:
1. **Remember and use personal details** from earlier lessons, such as the learner's name, job, family and upcoming events: "Last time you said you had a job interview. How did it go?" (Harmer's "recognising"; Tsui's contingency.)
2. **Be authentic, not sycophantic** (Scrivener/Rogers). React genuinely to content and show curiosity. Avoid formulaic superlatives; they also close down learner turns (Waring).
3. **Demand High micro-moves** after a successful answer:
   - "Nice. Say it again, but add *why*."
   - "Can you say that in one sentence using *although*?"
   - "Try it once more, a bit faster."

   Use them sparingly with anxious learners.
4. **Dogme-style emergent language.**
   - Collect 2–3 phrases from the learner's own speech.
   - Reformulate each into a better version.
   - Have the learner use them again in the repeat task.
   - Save them to a personal phrasebook that feeds the progress screen and spaced review.
5. **Structure each lesson on Goh & Burns' cycle**, compressed to 20 minutes (timings are a design proposal, not an empirical optimum):
   1. Warm-up chat with rapport (2–3 min).
   2. Goal (30 s).
   3. Input and planning: tutor model plus key chunks (2 min).
   4. Task, attempt 1 (4–5 min).
   5. Language focus on the learner's own utterances (3 min).
   6. Task repeat, attempts 2–3 (4 min).
   7. Reflection and progress screen with the next goal (2 min).
6. **Facilitator stance** (Underhill). Watch for anxiety cues such as long silences, very short answers or switching to Russian, and respond by lowering demand (easier questions, more scaffolding, reassurance) before raising it again.

### Gaps
- Could not verify exact wording from Harmer, Scrivener, Underhill or Thornbury books and blogs in-session, including Thornbury's blog entry on teacher talk.
- No empirical study was found that directly compares expert and novice teachers' **one-to-one speaking lessons**, which are closer to AI tutoring than whole-class lessons.
- No evidence was found on whether rapport behaviours transfer to an AI persona, for example whether remembering details raises enjoyment or retention with an AI.

## 6. Does speaking with AI reduce anxiety? (brief; covered in depth by another researcher)

### Takeaway
Early experimental evidence, including a randomised study published in *System* in 2024, shows AI-mediated speaking practice improving speaking and WTC. Conversational agents built with communication strategies and supportive backchannels raise WTC. Direct evidence on **anxiety reduction** is thinner, and a distinct "digital" communication anxiety exists. The AI therefore has to be **designed** to feel non-judgmental; this cannot be assumed.

### Cited Findings
- [RCT] **AI-mediated speaking tasks.** Fathi, Rahimi & Derakhshan (2024), *System* 121: 103254, a true experiment with randomisation. AI speaking tasks **beat traditional instruction** in improving EFL learners' speaking skills and WTC. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X24000368); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/378032201_Improving_EFL_learners'_speaking_skills_and_willingness_to_communicate_via_artificial_intelligence-mediated_interactions)
- [META] **AI for speaking and listening.** A meta-analysis in *Contemporary Educational Technology* reports a substantial overall effect of AI-enhanced instruction, with notable speaking gains. Figures were not retrieved. [CEDTECH](https://cedtech.editorialpark.com/article/the-effectiveness-of-artificial-intelligence-in-english-instruction-for-speaking-and-listening-17310)
- [EXP] [unverified] **Supportive conversational agent.** Ayedoun, Hayashi & Seta (2019), *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education* 29(1). An embodied conversational agent that used **communication strategies** (simplification, reformulation, time-gaining) and **affective backchannels** (encouragement, empathy) raised Japanese learners' WTC more than versions without them. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-018-0171-6)
- [META, low-credibility venue] **Technology-based interventions.** One speaking-anxiety meta-analysis found them more effective than traditional ones. [theusajournals.com](https://theusajournals.com/index.php/ijp/article/view/7131) Other meta-analyses found **no intervention-type moderator**. [Xiong et al. 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X241291258); [IJOPR](https://www.ijopr.com/article/the-effect-of-intervention-studies-on-foreign-language-speaking-anxiety-a-meta-analysis-study-16361)
- **Recent work located but not accessed:**
  - [2026 arXiv paper on reducing foreign language anxiety with level-appropriate embodied conversational agents](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.21887);
  - [*Annual Review of Applied Linguistics* paper modelling WTC from face-to-face vs digital L2 communication anxiety](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/modeling-efl-learners-willingness-to-communicate-the-roles-of-facetoface-and-digital-l2-communication-anxiety/3414896607A2536A9C882CA6EC39F30C);
  - [arXiv analysis of dialogue-act patterns in learner–chatbot oral practice](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.05702);
  - [AI intervention on EFL boredom (Springer chapter)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-30199-4_7).

### Inferences
- **Why the private setting should help.** A private AI partner removes the peer audience, which targets fear of negative evaluation, a core component of foreign language anxiety (Horwitz). This is theoretically well-motivated, and early experiments on WTC support it. It is a legitimate product promise ("practise without being judged"), but should not be marketed as a proven "cure for the language barrier".
- **Build in what Ayedoun et al. built:**
  - affective backchannels ("That's a good point", "I understand");
  - simplification or reformulation when the learner struggles;
  - modelling of time-gaining phrases.
- **Watch for "digital anxiety" triggers** in voice AI: latency, the AI talking over the learner, and mis-recognition of accented speech.
  - Always frame recognition failures as the system's fault: "Sorry, I didn't catch that. Could you say it again?" Never frame them as the learner's pronunciation failure.
  - Let the learner interrupt the AI (barge-in).

### Gaps
- Few rigorous studies isolate **anxiety** (as opposed to WTC or speaking scores) as an outcome of speech-to-speech AI practice. None were found for **Russian-speaking adults** or for **20-minute voice-only lessons**.
- It is unknown whether the anxiety reduction from AI practice **transfers** to speaking with humans, which is the learner's real goal.
- Details of the 2026 embodied-agent paper and the digital-anxiety paper were not accessible and should be checked by the dedicated AI-chatbot researcher.
