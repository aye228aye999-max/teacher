## 1. Nation's four strands: how to balance time in a lesson and across a course

### Takeaway
Nation's framework says a well-designed course gives roughly equal time (≈25% each) to four strands: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development. An activity counts as "fluency development" only under four conditions: familiar material, pressure to go faster, a large quantity of language, and a focus on meaning. The equal split is a design principle backed by converging research. No experiment has tested the split itself. For a 20-minute speaking lesson, the practical reading is to balance the strands at the level of the week or course, pushing input and item-learning into homework.

### Cited Findings
**Framework (THEORY; the evidence behind each strand converges, but no RCT has tested the time split)**
- [THEORY, 2007 (older)] Nation, P. (2007). *The four strands*. Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching, 1(1). Course activities can be classified into meaning-focused input (MFI), meaning-focused output (MFO), language-focused learning (LFL) and fluency development (FD). "In a well designed course there should be an even balance of these strands with roughly equal amounts of time given to each strand." The evidence Nation cites draws on the input hypothesis and learning from extensive reading (MFI), the output hypothesis (MFO), research on form-focused instruction (LFL), and research on the development of speaking and reading fluency (FD). — [Nation 2007, T&F abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2167/illt039.0); [author PDF, Victoria University of Wellington](https://www.victoria.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/1626121/2007-Four-strands.pdf); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Four-Strands-Nation/3f2756daf68ef11b69b98e002655591a144e9ccf)
- [THEORY, 2007 (older)] The MFO strand is "learning through speaking and writing". Like MFI, it involves largely incidental learning. — [Nation 2007 PDF](https://www.victoria.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/1626121/2007-Four-strands.pdf)
- [THEORY] Nation's four conditions for the fluency strand:
  1. Everything the learner listens to, reads, says or writes is largely familiar: no unfamiliar language features and no largely unfamiliar content.
  2. There is some pressure or encouragement to perform faster than usual.
  3. There is a large amount of input or output.
  4. The learner's focus is on receiving or conveying meaning.

  — [TESOL Union paper, "The Relevance of Nation's Four Strands Model to Online Teaching"](https://tesolunion.org/attachments/files/2YTA27YTCY6NZK36Y2FI6ZDC06M2YWEODDM6NZUYAMDFHDNTDMEOGY58MJFMAYTUY6NZYYAZTRH8MTA41OTZHDY2E37LJGXEMZGW8MZE36LJLL.pdf); [English Teaching Forum 63(3), pp. 2–8 (PDF)](https://americanenglish.state.gov/files/ae/resource_files/63_3_p02-08.pdf)
- [PRAC] Practitioners turn this into "≈25% of course time" per strand. The aim is that no strand is neglected. — [TESL Ontario *Contact*: Applying the Four Strands Framework in LINC classrooms](http://contact.teslontario.org/applying-the-four-strands-framework-in-linc-classrooms/)
- [PRAC] Independent learners use the strands to audit their own study time, which is directly relevant to app-based self-study. — [Hacking Chinese: balance your learning with Nation's four strands](https://www.hackingchinese.com/analyse-and-balance-your-chinese-learning-with-paul-nations-four-strands/); [Sinosplice: The four strands of a balanced language course](https://www.sinosplice.com/life/archives/2019/06/12/the-four-strands-of-a-balanced-language-course)
- [THEORY, 2012 (older)] Nation & Yamamoto (2012), *Applying the four strands to language learning*, extends the framework to individual learners. The search surfaced the PDF, but I could not retrieve its content. — [PDF](https://www.victoria.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0003/1626123/2012-Yamamoto-Four-strands.pdf)

**Evidence behind each strand verified this session (details in §3–§6)**
- MFI: extensive reading gives d = 0.57 against controls and d = 0.79 pre to post. — [Jeon & Day 2016, RFL](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1117026.pdf)
- FD: the 4/3/2 activity raises speech rate and cuts hesitations. — [Arevart & Nation 1991](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/003368829102200106); [Boers 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0033688214546964)
- LFL: pronunciation instruction gives d = 0.80–0.89. — [Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/36/3/345/2422438). Spaced practice beats massed practice. — [Kim & Webb 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12479)

### Inferences
- **Level of balance.** The 25% rule is a heuristic for a course or a week. A 20-minute voice lesson cannot give meaningful time to all four strands. A proposed in-lesson split, **as a design hypothesis only**:
  - ~40% MFO: tasks and role-plays
  - ~25–30% FD: timed retells of familiar content
  - ~20% LFL: chunks, pronunciation, feedback
  - ~10–15% MFI: the tutor's models and short listening

  Homework then carries most MFI (graded listening and reading) and most item-level LFL (spaced repetition, pronunciation micro-drills), so weekly totals come close to parity.
- **Example weekly budget.** 3 lessons × 20 min plus 5–6 homework sessions × 10–12 min ≈ 2 hours a week, or about 30 minutes per strand. The app can log minutes per strand per learner and rebalance automatically. For example, if LFL goes above ~25% of the week, it can shift the learner toward fluency or input.
- **Why weight lessons toward output.** A2–B1 learners who "understand but can't speak" have usually had a lot of MFI and LFL at school and little MFO or FD. Weighting lessons toward output and fluency is Nation's balance logic applied to this learner's history (an inference).
- **Building fluency activities in the AI.** They must meet all four conditions:
  - Recycle only language the learner has already met: chunks from earlier lessons and the learner's own earlier answers.
  - Add visible speed pressure, such as a countdown or a shrinking time limit.
  - Keep a real communicative purpose.
  - Maximise talk volume, with no corrections during the fluency round.

### Gaps
- I found no experimental test of the equal-time split. It remains a design principle.
- I could not verify whether Nation requires the balance per lesson or across a course, or what his exact wording on capping LFL is.
- **Unverified leads:**
  - Nation's writings are commonly paraphrased as saying balance is achieved over a course and that LFL should take no more than about a quarter of the time.
  - Newton & Nation, *Teaching ESL/EFL Listening and Speaking* (2nd ed., Routledge, 2021; 1st ed. Nation & Newton 2009) contains activity catalogues for each strand. Not accessed.
- Speed reading, a fluency-strand activity: an ERIC full text on "speed reading courses and their effect on…" surfaced but was not read. — [ERIC EJ887882](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887882.pdf)

## 2. Controlled to free practice: drilling, skill acquisition theory (DeKeyser) and proceduralisation

### Takeaway
Skill acquisition theory is the main theoretical warrant for sequencing practice from controlled to free. Explicit, declarative knowledge becomes procedural and then automatic through practice, and practice effects are **skill-specific**: to get better at speaking, learners must practise speaking. The empirical evidence I could verify is indirect but consistent. Controlled practice with explicit feedback produces the largest measured gains, but those gains transfer less to spontaneous speech, so every drill sequence should end in free, meaning-focused use. I found no direct meta-analytic evidence on specific drill formats (choral, substitution, back-chaining). They remain practitioner consensus.

### Cited Findings
**Theory**
- [THEORY, 2025] DeKeyser, R. M., & Suzuki, Y. (2025). Skill acquisition theory. In B. VanPatten, G. D. Keating & S. Wulff (Eds.), *Theories in Second Language Acquisition* (4th ed., pp. 157–182). Routledge. This is the current statement of the theory. — [academia.edu listing](https://www.academia.edu/136849439/DeKeyser_R_M_and_Suzuki_Y_2025_Skill_acquisition_theory_In_B_VanPatten_G_D_Keating_and_S_Wulff_Eds_Theories_in_second_language_acquisition_An_introduction_4th_ed_pp_157_182_Routledge); [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393631114_DeKeyser_R_M_Suzuki_Y_2025_Skill_acquisition_theory_In_B_VanPatten_G_D_Keating_S_Wulff_Eds_Theories_in_second_language_acquisition_An_introduction_4th_ed_pp_157-182_Routledge); [DeKeyser, "Automatization, Skill Acquisition, and Practice in SLA", Encyclopedia of Applied Linguistics](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781405198431.wbeal0067)
- [THEORY] The three stages:
  - **Declarative:** the learner acquires knowledge about the language and uses it to work out performance.
  - **Procedural:** procedural knowledge "dramatically facilitates performance routines".
  - **Automatic:** routines run "as a reflex".

  The theory frames explicit learning as including deliberate practice, noticing and hypothesis testing. — [Taie 2014, TPLS review (older)](https://www.academypublication.com/issues/past/tpls/vol04/09/30.pdf); [SSLA: "Testing the three-stage model of second language skill acquisition"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/testing-the-threestage-model-of-second-language-skill-acquisition/DF879921EDE594E795CBD8C18A87E86E) (findings not retrieved)
- [THEORY/PRAC] Instructional pathway as summarised in secondary sources: declarative knowledge comes from instruction and guided practice; it is proceduralised through **communicative drills that link form and meaning**; it becomes automatic through **extensive meaning-focused communication**. — [Taie 2014](https://www.academypublication.com/issues/past/tpls/vol04/09/30.pdf); [Logos conference paper on SAT](https://archive.logos-science.com/index.php/conference-proceedings/article/download/3046/3091/3124)
- [THEORY] The theory predicts that "the effects of instruction are skill-based" (skill specificity). — [DeKeyser & Suzuki 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393631114_DeKeyser_R_M_Suzuki_Y_2025_Skill_acquisition_theory_In_B_VanPatten_G_D_Keating_S_Wulff_Eds_Theories_in_second_language_acquisition_An_introduction_4th_ed_pp_157-182_Routledge); [Suzuki & DeKeyser, "Explicit knowledge and skill acquisition in L2 learning", Encyclopedia of Applied Linguistics 2nd ed. (in press)](https://www.academia.edu/136849366/Suzuki_Y_and_DeKeyser_R_M_in_press_Explicit_knowledge_and_skill_acquisition_in_second_language_learning_In_C_Chapelle_Ed_Encyclopedia_of_Applied_Linguistics_2nd_ed_Oxford_UK_Wiley)

**Indirect empirical evidence on controlled practice and transfer**
- [META, 2015 (older)] Pronunciation instruction effects were larger for treatments that gave **feedback**, for **longer** interventions and for **more controlled outcome measures**. — [Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015, Applied Linguistics 36(3): 345–366](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/36/3/345/2422438)
- [META, 2019] Pronunciation instruction is most effective when it targets specific features (segmentals, prosody, fluency) and gains are measured with **controlled** tasks such as word and sentence reading. It is less effective when measured on spontaneous speech. Gains from controlled practice therefore do not automatically reach free speech. — [Saito & Plonsky 2019, Language Learning](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12345); [ERIC EJ1224106](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1224106)
- [META, 2023/2024] ASR practice **with explicit corrective feedback** had a large effect (g = 0.86, 8 studies). ASR "dictation" without explicit correction was only moderately effective. — [Ngo, Chen et al., ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)
- [EXP, 2014 (older)] When learners repeated monologues, a "strikingly high amount of verbatim duplication of word strings" from their first version appeared in later versions. This explains the fluency gains, since learners retrieve ready-made sequences, and also the lack of improvement on other measures. — [Boers 2014, RELC Journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0033688214546964)

### Inferences
Proposed micro-cycle for each target chunk or structure, about 3–5 minutes (design inference for AI prompts):
1. **Model in context.** The AI uses the target in a short, meaningful exchange about the learner.
2. **Listen and repeat.** One learner, so no choral drilling. A "say it with me" shadowing mode can stand in for choral repetition. For long utterances, use **back-chaining**: build from the end of the sentence backwards to keep natural stress and intonation. This is a practitioner technique whose source I could not verify.
3. **Meaningful substitution.** The frame is fixed and the learner supplies personally true content, e.g. "I've been working at ___ for ___". Form is controlled; meaning is the learner's choice.
4. **Communicative drill.** The AI asks real questions, with an information gap, that call for the target.
5. **Free use.** A task where the target is useful but not forced. The AI logs whether the learner uses it spontaneously.
6. **Retrieval.** The target returns at the start of the next lesson and in homework.

Supporting rules:
- **Feedback policy by phase.** In controlled phases, give explicit, immediate feedback with a model and a retry (supported by Lee et al. 2015 and Ngo et al.). In free and fluency phases, give minimal or delayed feedback so as not to break fluency; see the accuracy trade-off under time pressure in §3.
- **Modality.** Because of skill specificity, practise in the target modality. For speaking goals, the learner should produce aloud rather than tap or choose.
- **Measure success in spontaneous speech,** not in drill accuracy (Saito & Plonsky 2019).
- **Keep mechanical repetition short.** Move to meaning-based drills within a few accurate repetitions. This is an inference; the critique of mechanical drills is only an unverified lead below.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis comparing drill formats (choral or individual, substitution, back-chaining). I could not retrieve British Council or Cambridge teacher guidance.
- **Unverified leads:**
  - Paulston (1970): typology of mechanical, meaningful and communicative drills.
  - Wong & VanPatten (2003), *Foreign Language Annals*, "The evidence is IN: Drills are OUT": critique of mechanical drills.
  - DeKeyser (1997), SSLA 19: learning curves follow the power law of practice; comprehension practice and production practice are skill-specific.
  - Li & DeKeyser (2017), SSLA: perception practice and production practice for Mandarin tone words produced skill-specific gains.
  - De Jong & Perfetti (2011), *Language Learning* 61(2): repeating the same topic in 4/3/2-style training produced fluency gains that transferred to a new topic, interpreted as proceduralisation.
  - Suzuki, Nakata & DeKeyser (2019), *Modern Language Journal*: desirable-difficulty framework for L2 practice.
  - Nakata & Suzuki (2019), MLJ: interleaved grammar exercises gave better delayed retention than blocked ones.
  - Sato & McDonough (2019), SSLA: quality of contextualised practice.
  - Lyster & Sato (2013): skill acquisition theory and classroom practice.
- I did not research PPP versus TBLT comparisons in this session (e.g., Shintani 2013; Li, Ellis & Zhu 2016; the Bryfonski & McKay 2019 TBLT meta-analysis). An adjacent researcher may cover them.

## 3. Fluency development: 4/3/2, task repetition and pre-task planning, with measured effects on complexity, accuracy and fluency

### Takeaway
Repeating the same talk under shrinking time (4/3/2) reliably increases speed and reduces hesitation, but it does **not** improve accuracy, and time pressure can suppress accuracy gains. A 2025 three-level meta-analysis of task repetition in speaking found **substantial** effects on syntactic complexity and accuracy and **smaller** effects on fluency and lexical complexity. Effects were larger in foreign-language contexts (like Russia), larger for narrative tasks and exact repetition, and **smaller when repetitions were spaced further apart**. Pre-task planning (Johnson & Abdi Tabari 2022 meta-analysis) appears to have moderate effects on syntactic complexity and fluency but small or no effects on accuracy and lexis; this characterisation comes from a secondary summary. Unpressured "online" planning helps accuracy (Yuan & Ellis 2003).

### Cited Findings
**4/3/2 technique**
- [PRAC/EXP] Procedure: the learner gives the **same talk to three different listeners**, first in 4 minutes, then 3, then 2. It was first used by Maurice (1983), then by Nation (1989) and Arevart & Nation (1991). — [4/3/2 with EFL learners in China (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271258299_The_development_of_speaking_fluency_The_432_technique_for_the_EFL_learners_in_China); [TESL-EJ 26(102): 4/3/2 plus self-assessment](https://tesl-ej.org/wordpress/issues/volume26/ej102/ej102a1/)
- [EXP, 1991 (older)] Arevart & Nation, "Fluency improvement in a second language", *RELC Journal* 22(1). They replicated Nation (1989) with **20 intermediate ESL learners in New Zealand**. By the **third** delivery, hesitations had dropped significantly and speech rate was much faster. The search did not surface the exact words-per-minute figures. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/003368829102200106); secondary summary: [ResearchGate China 4/3/2 study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271258299_The_development_of_speaking_fluency_The_432_technique_for_the_EFL_learners_in_China)
- [EXP, 2014 (older)] Boers, "A reappraisal of the 4/3/2 activity", *RELC Journal*.
  - Design: **10 adult ESL learners**, each delivering **two talks three times**, one talk under increasing time pressure and one without.
  - Fluency improved in **both** conditions.
  - The potential of repetition to improve **accuracy was compromised by the increasing time pressure**.
  - Heavy verbatim duplication of the learners' own word strings explains both the fluency gains and the lack of other gains.

  — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0033688214546964); [ERIC EJ1047012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1047012)
- [EXP, online 2021 / print 2024] Tran & Saito, "Effects of the 4/3/2 activity revisited: Extending Boers (2014) and Thai & Boers (2016)", *Language Teaching Research* 28(2): 326–345. A search summary attributes to this paper the finding that 4/3/2 "significantly promoted certain aspects of fluency (but not accuracy) development, regardless of the interval conditions". The same wording is attributed to the 2025 System follow-up below, so which paper it belongs to is uncertain **[SEC]**. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effects-of-the-4-3-2-activity-revisited:-Extending-Tran-Saito/ca261f9b860ce2e01f68e23f962ed31f6eb851da); [author PDF](http://kazuyasaito.net/LTR2021.pdf)
- [EXP, 2025] "Effects of the 4/3/2 activity revisited: Aptitude matters, but distributed practice does not", *System* (authors not retrieved).
  - Design: **50 Chinese learners of English**, split evenly between **massed** and **short-spaced** schedules; collected fully online on the Gorilla platform.
  - 4/3/2 improved certain aspects of **fluency but not accuracy**, **regardless of spacing**.
  - Fluency gains related to linguistic encoding were associated with **auditory processing** (perceptual acuity), **not working memory**.

  — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X2500346X); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398073071_Limited_Effects_of_Distributed_Practice_on_L2_Proficiency_in_the_432_Activity_Roles_of_Aptitude_in_Predicting_Fluency_and_Accuracy)

**Task repetition**
- [META, 2025] "Task repetition and L2 oral performance: A meta-analysis", *System* (Oct 2025; authors and numeric effect sizes not retrieved). It used **three-level meta-analytic models** across complexity, accuracy, lexis and fluency.
  - Task repetition had a **substantial impact on syntactic complexity and accuracy**: learners produced more complex, less error-prone utterances.
  - **Fluency and lexical complexity** also improved, but gains were **smaller**. The authors conclude that repetition "primarily enhances structural aspects of language".
  - Moderators:
    - **Larger** effects on syntactic complexity, accuracy and fluency in **foreign-language** than in second-language contexts.
    - **Narrative tasks** and **exact repetition** were particularly effective for syntactic complexity.
    - **Longer spacing intervals were associated with smaller effects** on syntactic complexity and fluency.

  — [ScienceDirect](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X25002787); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398171158_Task_repetition_and_L2_oral_performance_A_meta-analysis); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/44fd381f97281b1e9cdc33a9834e78ca4724e36a)
- [META, 2025, writing, for contrast] A meta-analysis of task repetition in L2 **writing** covered 17 independent studies and 442 participants. It found a **medium** effect on fluency, **small** effects on accuracy and lexical complexity, and a minimal, non-significant effect on syntactic complexity. Repetition interval, genre and medium were moderators. — [Journal of Second Language Writing / ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1060374325000803); companion writing meta-analysis: [Liu & Tang 2025, LTR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688251381268)
- [SR, 2025] Rogers & Li, "The time between tasks in task repetition research: A systematic review", *LTR*. It synthesised **107 studies** on how spacing between repetitions has been implemented, how many times tasks were repeated, and how researchers justified those choices. The authors frame this as a methodological gap: spacing is rarely varied deliberately. — [SAGE / DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688251323047)

**Pre-task and online planning**
- [META, 2022] Johnson, M. D., & Abdi Tabari, M. (2022). Task planning and oral L2 production: A research synthesis and meta-analysis. *Applied Linguistics*, 43(6), 1143–1164 (doi:10.1093/applin/amac026). A secondary summary **[SEC]** reports that across **36 studies**, strategic pre-task planning had **moderate positive effects on syntactic complexity and fluency** and **small or no effects on lexical complexity and accuracy**. A different summary describes the planning literature as "inconclusive", with no findings "consistently and unambiguously" confirming a positive link. That probably describes the state of the literature before the meta-analysis, but the two summaries conflict and exact g values should be verified. — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/43/6/1143/6682841); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363245811_Task_Planning_and_Oral_L2_Production_A_Research_Synthesis_and_Meta-analysis)
- [EXP, 2003 (older)] Yuan, F., & Ellis, R. (2003). *Applied Linguistics* 24(1).
  - **Pre-task planning** increased grammatical complexity.
  - **Online planning** (unpressured time during the task) improved **accuracy** and grammatical complexity.
  - Pre-task planners were **more fluent and lexically varied** than online planners.

  — [ERIC EJ662877](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ662877); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/24/1/1/167573)
- [PRAC/SEC] In most planning studies, learners had **about 10 minutes** of planning time. — [TESL-EJ: Investigating the effects of planning time](https://www.tesl-ej.org/pdf/ej81/a3.pdf)

**Summary of verified directions of effect**

| Technique | Fluency | Accuracy | Complexity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4/3/2 (same talk, shrinking time) | ↑ speed, ↓ hesitation | no gain; time pressure suppresses it | not established | Arevart & Nation 1991; Boers 2014; System 2025 |
| Task repetition (overall) | ↑ (smaller) | ↑ (substantial) | syntactic ↑ (substantial); lexical ↑ (smaller) | System 2025 meta-analysis |

### Inferences
- **"Shrinking retell" (4/3/2 scaled to a 20-minute A2–B1 lesson).** Same content three times at, for example, 90 s → 60 s → 45 s, or 2:00 → 1:30 → 1:00 for stronger B1 learners. To keep a genuine listener, the AI switches persona each round ("Now tell my colleague, who's in a hurry"). Show a visible timer and **do not correct during the rounds**. These durations are untested.
- **Pair speed with accuracy.** After the speed rounds, add one untimed "careful" version with feedback on at most 1–2 priority errors. When the lesson's goal is accuracy or complexity, use repetition **without** time pressure. This follows the trade-off in Boers 2014 and System 2025.
- **Repeat soon, then vary.** Longer gaps shrink repetition effects (2025 meta-analysis). So repeat the exact task within the lesson, run it again in homework within about 1–3 days, and only later move to "procedural" repetition (same task type, new content) for transfer. The evidence for procedural repetition is unverified.
- **Prefer narrative tasks** (retell a story, a weekend, a work incident) as repetition vehicles, since narrative repetition was particularly effective for syntactic complexity.
- **Planning in a 20-minute lesson.** The research norm of about 10 minutes does not fit. Give 30–60 seconds of guided planning, with 3–4 key chunks or ideas on screen, before monologic tasks. Expected benefit is mainly fluency and complexity. For accuracy-focused turns, let the learner speak without a time limit ("online planning"). Very short planning times have not been tested; see Gaps.
- **Context advantage.** Russian speakers learning in Russia or the CIS are in a foreign-language context, where repetition effects were larger. That supports heavy use of task repetition.
- **Individual differences.** Auditory processing predicted fluency gains from 4/3/2 (System 2025) and predicts pronunciation learning ([Zheng, Saito & Tierney 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0267658320978493)). Learners who progress slowly may benefit from perception or listening training (inference).
- **Track fluency automatically.** Measure utterance fluency every lesson from ASR timestamps: speech rate, mean length of run, pause frequency and length, filled pauses. Show learners the trend. This also documents time spent in the fluency strand.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve numeric effect sizes or authors for the 2025 System task-repetition meta-analysis, or exact g values for Johnson & Abdi Tabari 2022. The 2025 meta-analysis may be the published version of Wang's SSRN preprint "A Meta-Analysis of the Effectiveness of Task Repetition on L2 Oral Output" ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4828952)); unconfirmed.
- I found no evidence on **very short planning times** (under 1 minute) of the kind an app lesson allows.
- Delayed and transfer effects of 4/3/2 on new topics were not verified.
- **Unverified leads:**
  - Nation (1989), *System* 17: the original 4/3/2 study.

## 4. Pronunciation: instruction effects, intelligibility and comprehensibility vs accent, priorities, and ASR/CAPT feedback

### Takeaway
Pronunciation instruction works, with large pooled effects (d = 0.89 within groups and 0.80 between groups). Effects are larger with **feedback**, **longer interventions** and **controlled** outcome measures (Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015). Gains shown in spontaneous speech, and in comprehensibility or intelligibility, are much rarer (Thomson & Derwing 2015; Saito & Plonsky 2019). ASR-based practice has a medium overall effect (g = 0.69) and a large effect with **explicit corrective feedback** (g = 0.86). It is strong for **segmentals**, weak for **suprasegmentals**, ineffective over **short** durations, and weaker when learners practise **alone**. I could not verify the intelligibility-versus-nativeness literature (Munro & Derwing, Levis, Jenkins's Lingua Franca Core) this session; see Gaps.

### Cited Findings
- [META, 2015 (older)] Lee, J., Jang, J., & Plonsky, L. (2015). The effectiveness of second language pronunciation instruction: A meta-analysis. *Applied Linguistics*, 36(3), 345–366.
  - 86 unique reports.
  - N-weighted **d = 0.89** (within-group) and **d = 0.80** (between-group).
  - Larger effects for (i) **longer interventions**, (ii) treatments that **provide feedback**, (iii) **more controlled outcome measures**.

  — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/36/3/345/2422438); [ERIC EJ1067987](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1067987); [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-pronunciation-instruction-a-/)
- [SR, 2015 (older)] Thomson, R. I., & Derwing, T. M. (2015). The effectiveness of L2 pronunciation instruction: A narrative review. *Applied Linguistics*, 36(3), 326ff.
  - **75 studies**. Results were mixed despite many reports of significant improvement.
  - Although the field has moved toward intelligibility and comprehensibility, **most studies still promoted native-like targets**.
  - Many studies used computer-assisted pronunciation teaching (CAPT). Segmentals were studied more often than suprasegmentals.
  - The review attributes divergent outcomes to learner individual differences, the goals and focus of instruction, the type and duration of input, and how outcomes were assessed.

  — [ERIC EJ1067998](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1067998); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/36/3/326/2422540); [University of Bristol record](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-l2-pronunciation-instruction-a-narrative-rev/)

  - **15 studies, 38 effect sizes (2008–2021)**; overall **g = 0.69** (medium).
  - ASR **with explicit corrective feedback**: large effect, **g = 0.86** (8 studies). **ASR dictation**: moderate.
  - **Large** effect on **segmental** pronunciation; **small** on **suprasegmental**.
  - **Medium-to-long** treatment duration gave better outcomes. **Short duration** showed **no differential effect** compared with non-ASR conditions.

- [EXP, 2025] In 4/3/2 training, fluency gains were associated with auditory processing, not working memory (§3). — [System 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X2500346X)

### Inferences
- **Train narrowly, then transfer.**
  - Train each target feature in short controlled drills with **explicit** feedback: what was wrong, a model, a retry.
  - Immediately require the feature in a free sentence or answer. Controlled-task gains are the largest and the least transferable (Lee et al. 2015; Saito & Plonsky 2019).
  - Judge success by comprehensibility in spontaneous speech, for example a monthly 1-minute monologue rated for comprehensibility.
- **Few targets, sustained over weeks.** Longer interventions produce larger effects, and short ASR treatments showed no advantage. So keep 1–2 pronunciation targets per week and keep them active for several weeks, rather than running one-off pronunciation lessons.
- **ASR and AI feedback design.**
  - Explicit correction beats "dictation" feedback that only shows a transcript (g = 0.86 vs moderate).
  - Start with **segmentals**, where ASR works best. Handle suprasegmentals (stress, rhythm, intonation) through modelling and imitation or shadowing, since ASR effects there are small. The shadowing evidence is an unverified lead (§6).
  - Solo ASR practice had small effects and peer practice large ones. Embedding pronunciation work **inside dialogue** with the AI interlocutor, rather than in isolated word lists, is a plausible way to approximate the peer condition. This is a hypothesis to A/B test.
- **Recognition reliability.** Accented speech raises the risk of false error flags. Suppress corrections when recognition confidence is low. This is an inference; a 2025 arXiv paper on ASR for non-native English surfaced but was not read: [arXiv 2503.06924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06924).
- **Priorities.** Pending verification of the intelligibility literature below, target features that cause comprehension breakdowns rather than accent per se, and never grade learners on nativeness.

## 5. Vocabulary and formulaic language: chunks, frequency-based selection, retrieval practice, spacing and spaced-repetition parameters

### Takeaway
- **Retrieve, don't re-study.** Repeated testing after an item is learned produces large gains in delayed recall; repeated study produces none (Karpicke & Roediger 2008).
- **Space, and space longer for longer retention.** In L2 learning, spaced practice beats massed practice, and longer spacing beats shorter spacing at longer retention intervals. Expanding versus equal intervals makes no clear difference (Kim & Webb 2022, 48 experiments, N = 3,411).
- **Size gaps to the retention goal.** The optimal gap is about 20–40% of a 1-week retention goal and about 5–10% of a 1-year goal (Cepeda et al. 2008).
- **Retrieve each item five or more times per session.** Five or seven retrievals beat one or three (Nakata 2017).
- **Teach chunks.** Getting learners to notice formulaic sequences raised blind-rated oral proficiency (Boers et al. 2006).

### Cited Findings
**Retrieval practice (testing effect)**
- [EXP, 2008 (older)] Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. *Science*, 319, 966–968. Students learned foreign-language vocabulary. Once a learner recalled an item correctly, it was either repeatedly studied but dropped from testing, repeatedly tested but dropped from study, or dropped from both. **Repeated studying after learning had no effect on delayed recall; repeated testing had a large positive effect.** — [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1152408); [PubMed 18276894](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276894/); [author PDF (WashU)](http://psychnet.wustl.edu/memory/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Karpicke-Roediger-2008_Sci.pdf)

**Spacing**
- [EXP, large-scale, 2008 (older)] Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological Science*, 19(11), 1095–1102.
  - **More than 1,350 participants** learned facts, reviewed them after a gap of **up to 3.5 months**, and took a final test **up to 1 year** later.
  - At any test delay, lengthening the gap first **increased** and then gradually **reduced** final performance.

**Spacing reference table (computed from Cepeda et al. 2008's verified ratios; the arithmetic is mine)**

| Retention goal | Optimal gap, % of goal | Optimal gap, absolute |
|---|---|---|
| 1 week | 20–40% | ≈ 1.5–3 days |
| 1 year | 5–10% | ≈ 18–36 days |

Intermediate horizons (1–3 months) fall between these two points. Interpolating is an assumption.

### Inferences
- **Every review is a recall attempt.** The learner tries to **say** the chunk from a situational prompt or an L1 cue before hearing or seeing the answer. Never "review" by re-listening or re-reading (Karpicke & Roediger 2008). For speaking goals, retrieval should be oral, following skill specificity (§2).
- **Within a session.** Give each new chunk **about 5 or more spaced retrievals**, interleaved with other items, across the lesson and that day's homework (Nakata 2017).
- **Across sessions.**
  - First review about 1 day after the lesson.
  - Then gaps growing toward about 10–20% of the retention goal. For a 3-month course horizon, later gaps of roughly 1–2.5 weeks.
  - For items meant to last a year, later gaps of roughly 3–5 weeks.
  - Expanding or equal schedules both work (Kim & Webb 2022), so choose whichever maximises adherence and keeps the daily review load predictable.
  - Longer spacing only pays off if learners actually return, so pair it with reminders (§6).
- **Chunks first.** The lesson's input should explicitly highlight 3–5 formulaic sequences (noticing, per Boers et al. 2006). Recycle them in the lesson's fluency round and in homework retrieval, so they become the "duplicated strings" that carry fluency (Boers 2014).
- **Frequency-based selection** (unverified leads below): prioritise high-frequency spoken vocabulary and spoken multi-word expressions over low-frequency words, and select for the learner's use situations (work, travel, small talk).
- **Session length.** No verified guidance. A plausible default to test is 5–10 minutes a day, with about 5–10 new items per day capped so reviews don't pile up (inference).

## 6. Homework and independent practice: extensive reading and listening, shadowing, deliberate practice, homework length and frequency, habit formation, and the testing effect for grammar

### Takeaway
Of the independent-practice methods asked about, only **extensive reading** had meta-analytic evidence verified this session. ### Inferences
**Default homework block**: 10–15 minutes a day, 5–6 days a week. Distributed practice beats massed, so do not accept a weekly "catch-up" session as a substitute.
1. **Oral spaced-repetition retrieval, 3–5 minutes.** Chunks from recent lessons, on the §5 schedule. The learner speaks the answer before hearing it.
2. **Task re-run, 2–4 minutes.** Repeat the lesson's retell or monologue within 1–3 days. Once untimed with self-checking, then once timed and shorter. Short intervals preserve repetition effects, and massed vs short-spaced timing made no difference for 4/3/2, so the exact scheduling is flexible.
3. **Meaning-focused input, 5–10 minutes.** Graded audio or reading at an easy level, supported by the extensive reading meta-analysis (EFL effects larger). Pick texts on the topics of upcoming lessons so the input primes later output.
4. **Optional pronunciation micro-drill, 2–3 minutes.** One segmental target, explicit ASR feedback, kept up for weeks. Short treatments showed no effect.

**Weekly check**: a 1-minute recorded monologue on a familiar topic. Track speech rate, pauses and comprehensibility over time as a learner-facing progress metric.

**Recognising progress**: extensive reading gains come from sustained programmes, so the app should frame reading and listening as a months-long habit rather than a weekly task (inference).

### Gaps
- **Extensive listening:** no verified meta-analysis or study. Unverified leads: Chang & Millett (2014), *ELT Journal*, on extensive listening and L2 listening fluency; Renandya & Farrell (2011), *ELT Journal*, on "teacher, the tape is too fast!"; reading-while-listening studies.
- **Shadowing:** no meta-analysis verified. Unverified leads:
  - Hamada (2016), LTR 20(1): shadowing mainly benefits lower-proficiency learners' phoneme perception and listening.
  - Foote & McDonough (2017), *Journal of Second Language Pronunciation*: mobile shadowing over 8 weeks improved comprehensibility and fluency but not accent.
  - Kadota (2019), *Shadowing as a Practice in Second Language Acquisition* (Routledge).
- **Deliberate practice:** unverified lead Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science* 25(8). In their meta-analysis, deliberate practice explained about 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions. That is a caution against claims that "practice hours alone" drive outcomes.
- **Homework length and frequency for adults:** I found no adult-language-specific evidence. Unverified lead: Cooper, Robinson & Patall (2006), *Review of Educational Research* 76(1), a meta-analysis of the homework–achievement link, applies to school students only.
- **Habit formation:** unverified leads.
  - Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6): median 66 days to reach automaticity, range 18–254; missing a single day did not materially derail habit formation.
  - Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38: meta-analysis of implementation intentions ("if-then" plans) for goal attainment, d ≈ 0.65.
- **Testing effect for grammar:** unverified leads.
  - Rowland (2014), *Psychological Bulletin*: testing-effect meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.50.
  - Yang et al. (2021), *Psychological Bulletin*: classroom quizzing meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.50.
  - Pan & Rickard (2018), *Psychological Bulletin*: transfer of test-enhanced learning.
  - Nakata & Suzuki (2019), MLJ: interleaved grammar practice.
  - Suzuki (2017), *Language Learning*: optimal practice spacing for L2 morphology.

  I found no verified L2-grammar-specific retrieval-practice meta-analysis.
- **Mobile-assisted learning and AI speaking tutors:** meta-analyses on mobile-assisted language learning and on AI chatbots for speaking (2023–2026) were not searched. The web-search budget ran out first. These are highly relevant to the product and should be covered by an adjacent research pass.
