## Q1. Meta-analyses and reviews: overall effect sizes, durability, moderators

### Takeaway
Oral CF reliably helps L2 development. The effects are medium to large and last on delayed posttests: d = 0.61–0.64 across 33 studies (Li 2010); significant, durable effects in 15 classroom studies with N = 827 (Lyster & Saito 2010); mean d = 1.16 for oral and written CF combined (Russell & Spada 2006). In classrooms, prompts beat recasts. Implicit feedback holds up better over time than explicit feedback. The moderators do not agree across meta-analyses: setting, treatment length and age come out differently. No new comprehensive oral-CF meta-analysis for 2023–2026 turned up. Recent syntheses cover CF timing and ASR-based pronunciation training.

### Inferences
- The case for building CF into the tutor is strong. Every meta-analysis finds positive effects that last to delayed posttests. For adults the effects may be smaller than for children (Lyster & Saito's age moderator), but they are still positive.
- The app is effectively a one-to-one, lab-like setting: individual attention, salient feedback, one learner. Lab studies show larger effects than classroom studies (Li 2010). This suggests the effects achievable in the app sit toward the upper range. This is an analogy, not tested.
- Russian adults learning in Russia are foreign-language (EFL) learners, and Li (2010) found larger effects for FL learners. Together with the higher recast uptake Sheen (2004) found in EFL/ESL adult settings (Q2), this suggests the target users will notice and use CF.
- Explicit CF looks stronger in the short term, while implicit CF holds up longer (Li 2010). A hybrid fits this: brief implicit or prompt-based CF during conversation, plus a short explicit review at the end of the session.
- Lyster & Saito found the largest effects on free-production measures. The app's success metric should therefore include accuracy in free speech in later sessions, not just immediate repetition. Immediate uptake is not proof of learning (Salmi & Farvardin 2025).

## Q2. Types of oral feedback (Lyster & Ranta 1997 taxonomy): uptake, repair, and which types suit which targets and learners

### Takeaway
Teachers mostly use recasts: 55% of CF moves in Lyster & Ranta (1997), and 55–83% across four settings in Sheen (2004). In child immersion classrooms recasts produce the least learner repair: fewer than 20% of recasts led to repair. Prompts (elicitation, metalinguistic clue, clarification request, repetition) push learners to fix the error themselves, and they beat recasts for grammar. This holds especially for lower-proficiency learners (Ammar & Spada 2006). Recasts work better:
- when they are salient: stress, declarative intonation, one change (Loewen & Philp 2006);
- in adult EFL/ESL settings (Sheen 2004);
- for pronunciation and vocabulary, which learners recognise as correction. Grammar recasts are often heard as comments on meaning (Mackey, Gass & McDonough 2000).

Anxious learners may not benefit from recasts (Sheen 2008).

### Cited Findings

**Taxonomy (Lyster & Ranta 1997, *SSLA* 19: 37–66), standard definitions, paraphrased** — [ERIC EJ539354](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ539354); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/CORRECTIVE-FEEDBACK-AND-LEARNER-UPTAKE-Lyster-Ranta/eecbea6c9fdc3a79deb060123883a6de920c70fc); [table of categories](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Categorization-of-Oral-Corrective-Feedback-Lyster-and-Ranta-1997_tbl1_314418516)
- **Recast.** The teacher reformulates all or part of the learner's utterance without the error, usually without saying that an error occurred. This is implicit, input-providing feedback.
- **Explicit correction.** The teacher clearly signals the error and gives the correct form.
- **Elicitation.** The teacher draws the correct form out of the learner by:
  - pausing so the learner completes the utterance;
  - asking a question ("How do we say…?");
  - asking the learner to reformulate.
- **Metalinguistic feedback (clue).** The teacher comments on, gives information about, or asks about the form's correctness without giving the correct form.
- **Clarification request.** The teacher signals that the utterance was not understood or was ill-formed ("Pardon?").
- **Repetition.** The teacher repeats the error, usually with rising intonation.
- Elicitation, metalinguistic clue, clarification request and repetition together are called **"prompts"**: they withhold the answer and push the learner to self-repair. Lyster, Saito & Sato (2013) review this grouping. — [ERIC EJ1004287](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1004287)

**Uptake and repair: descriptive classroom evidence**
- [OBS] **Lyster & Ranta (1997)**, French immersion grades 4–5. Recasts made up **55%** of CF moves, elicitation **14%**, clarification requests **11%**. Of **375 recasts**:
  - **260** got no uptake (about 69%);
  - **49** got uptake without repair;
  - **66** led to repair, fewer than 20% (about 18%).

  **Elicitation was the most successful type at leading to repair.** Elicitation, metalinguistic feedback, clarification requests and repetition all led to student-generated repair more often than recasts. — [ERIC-hosted secondary study](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1134390.pdf); [review of a decade of research since L&R](https://l2aquisition.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/corrective-feedback-over-a-decade-of-research-since-lyster-and-ranta-1997-where-do-we-stand-today.pdf)

### Inferences (proposed decision rules for choosing a type)
- **Grammar (morphosyntax).** This covers tense, 3rd-person -s, articles, question word order, and "I am agree"-type errors.
  - A2–B1 learners and conversational recasts are a poor match here. Grammar recasts are often not noticed (Mackey et al. 2000). Lower-proficiency learners gain more from prompts (Ammar & Spada 2006).
  - Default when the learner is likely to **know** the form (it was taught before, or they have used it correctly): a **prompt**, i.e. elicitation or a metalinguistic clue.
  - Default when the form is probably **unknown** (new structure): **explicit correction plus one short rule**. A prompt cannot draw out knowledge the learner does not have. This fits Lyster, Saito & Sato's "initiate vs consolidate" distinction.
- **Vocabulary and pronunciation.** A short, stressed **recast** is usually enough, because learners recognise lexical and phonological feedback. For errors that keep coming back, escalate to explicit modelling plus a repetition request.
- **If you recast, make it salient.**
  - Change only one thing.
  - Stress the corrected word.
  - Use declarative intonation.
  - Keep it short.
  - Optionally give a second move (for example, a quick "went, yes" after the learner repeats).

  These follow Loewen & Philp (2006).
- **Anxious learners.**
  - Signals: long pauses, "sorry, my English is bad", switching to Russian, very short answers.
  - Response: reduce how often you correct during the conversation, and move most CF into the private end-of-session review.

  Sheen (2008) found high-anxiety learners did not benefit from recasts.
- **Do not demand uptake.** Leave a pause so the learner can self-correct, but do not insist they repeat. Uptake does not reliably predict learning (Salmi & Farvardin 2025).
- **Clarification requests are risky for a voice AI.** A fake "Sorry?" after an error the AI actually understood is ambiguous: the learner may think the audio failed. Keep clarification requests for genuine failures to understand, which also covers low ASR confidence.

### Gaps
- Most per-type uptake and repair data come from child French immersion classrooms (Lyster & Ranta 1997). Adult EFL data (Sheen 2004) show different recast uptake. I found no uptake data by CF type for Russian-L1 adults.
- Two relevant studies were not verified this session: [RECALLED – VERIFY]
  - Yang & Lyster (2010, *SSLA*): prompts beat recasts for **regular** past tense (rule-based); the two were roughly equal for irregular past tense (item-based).
  - Ellis, Loewen & Erlam (2006, *SSLA*): metalinguistic feedback beat recasts for past-tense -ed with adult learners.
- Rassaei (2015): recalled as finding that CF type interacts with anxiety level. The direction could not be verified.

## Q3. Timing: immediate vs delayed feedback; feedback during fluency vs accuracy activities

### Takeaway
The experimental evidence slightly favours **immediate** CF for learning grammar:
- Fu & Li (2022): immediate > delayed, 145 learners, English past tense.
- A 2023 systematic review of 20 studies: about half favoured immediate CF; the rest mostly found no difference, e.g. Quinn 2014 and Henderson 2021.
- In oral production, immediate CF beat delayed CF (Arroyo & Yilmaz 2017/2018).

Delayed CF still works, and no study found it clearly superior. Practitioner guidance recommends delayed correction during fluency work to protect the flow of the activity. Brief, unobtrusive immediate CF on a few priority targets, plus a delayed review of the rest, is consistent with both the experimental evidence and the practitioner guidance.

### Cited Findings

**Experimental and review evidence**
- [SR] **2023 systematic review**, "Optimal timing of treatment for errors in second language learning – A systematic review of corrective feedback timing" (PMC9995700). 20 studies.
  - "Half found immediate feedback outperformed delayed feedback in the post-tests or delayed post-tests, or both."
  - In oral communication, **Arroyo & Yilmaz (2017, 2018)** found "the immediate feedback group outperformed the delayed feedback group in oral production".
  - "The six studies providing **hybrid CF** tend to find that immediate feedback was more effective." My reading is that hybrid CF means combined types, such as a prompt followed by a recast.
  - **Quinn (2014)** and **Henderson (2021)** found **no difference** between immediate and delayed CF.

  — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9995700/)
- [EXP] **Fu & Li (2022)**, *SSLA* (an erratum was issued in 2022). 145 seventh-grade EFL learners, English past tense. Four groups:
  - Immediate CF, given in the **first** session;
  - Delayed CF, given in the **final** session;
  - Task-only;
  - Control.

  Measures were an untimed grammaticality judgment test and an elicited imitation test. Mixed-effects analyses showed that "**immediate CF was more facilitative of L2 development than delayed CF**". The authors stress "addressing linguistic errors **before they are proceduralized**". Note that "delayed" here means a later session, a longer delay than end-of-task. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-immediate-and-delayed-corrective-feedback-on-l2-development/B4B2D455749B752BD4F6DD636ACD688F); [ERIC EJ1330291](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1330291)
- [EXP] **Li, Zhu & Ellis (2016)**, *MLJ* 100(1): 276–295.
  - Participants: 120 Chinese middle-school EFL learners, 4 intact classes.
  - Target: the **English past passive**, a structure new to them.

  - CF form: **a prompt followed by a recast**.

### Inferences
- **Default policy for the AI tutor.** Give immediate CF only for:
  - (a) the session's target forms;
  - (b) errors that stop meaning getting through;
  - (c) errors that have already recurred.

  Keep each immediate move to one short line, then go back to the topic. Log everything else for a **delayed end-of-session review**: the top 3 errors, explicit correction, and quick practice.
- **Delay helps verification.** A delayed review gives the system time to double-check suspected errors before claiming them. For example, it can re-transcribe the stored audio with a slower, more accurate ASR model, or require agreement between two recognisers. This directly reduces false corrections (see Q6). It is a design inference.
- **Keep the delay short.** Fu & Li's delayed condition meant a later session, and it lost to immediate CF. So the review should happen at the end of the same conversation rather than the next day. An optional spaced "remember this?" in the next session would add retrieval practice.
- **Match timing to the mode.** In "free conversation / fluency" mode, keep immediate CF sparse and implicit. In "practice / accuracy" mode (drills, role-play with a target structure), immediate prompts are appropriate. This follows the practitioner accuracy/fluency distinction. Studies such as Arroyo & Yilmaz suggest immediate CF during communicative tasks is not harmful to learning.

## Q4. What to correct: target forms, global vs local errors, frequency and stigmatising errors, selective vs comprehensive correction, learner preferences, amount per turn

### Takeaway
Research and expert consensus favour **selective, focused** correction over correcting everything. Priorities are the lesson's target forms, errors that block understanding (global errors), and frequent or persistent errors. Learners, especially form-oriented foreign-language adults, **want more correction than teachers usually give**. The app should therefore agree an explicit "correction contract" with each user. No experimental study defines an optimal number of corrections per turn. However, single-change, short feedback works best (Loewen & Philp 2006), which supports **at most one correction per learner turn**.

### Inferences (proposed "what to correct" policy)
- **Priority ladder for immediate CF (highest first):**
  1. **Meaning-blocking (global) errors**, where the AI genuinely cannot tell what the learner meant. Handle these with a genuine clarification request or confirmation check. This is negotiating meaning, not "correcting".
  2. **The session's target structure or vocabulary** (focused CF).
  3. **Recurring errors.** The same error type appears 2 or more times in a session or across sessions, suggesting a stable, entrenched error rather than a one-off slip. Fu & Li's warning about errors becoming "proceduralized" (automatic) applies here.
  4. **High-visibility errors** that make an adult sound much less competent in work or travel settings, e.g. "I am agree", "he don't", "Where you live?". I found no empirical source for stigma rankings, so this rests on judgment (see Gaps).
  5. **Everything else goes to the delayed review only**, or is not mentioned at all.
- **Do NOT correct:**
  - slips the learner corrects themselves;
  - content, facts or opinions;
  - acceptable variants (British vs American forms; informal spoken forms such as "gonna" in casual chat);
  - accent features that do not affect intelligibility;
  - errors inside a quotation or a name;
  - anything the AI is not confident it heard correctly (see Q6).
- **Frequency caps (design proposal, not empirically calibrated):**
  - at most **1 immediate correction per learner turn**;
  - in free-conversation mode, a target of **no more than about 1 correction per 3 learner turns**;
  - **one error per correction move**; never bundle two fixes into one recast;
  - never more than **3 escalating moves** on the same error;
  - never correct twice in a row if the learner shows frustration.
- **Delayed review size:** **3 items** by default, up to 5 at B1 if the learner opts in. Rank items by priority, then frequency. Keep the full list available in a text log inside the app.
- **Correction contract.**
  - At onboarding, ask how much correction the learner wants during conversation (a lot / important errors only / only at the end).
  - Honour voice commands such as "correct me more" or "just let me talk".
  - Re-check preferences periodically.

  The default should lean toward *more* correction than a communicative-approach teacher would give (learners want more), but still selective: important errors during the conversation, everything else in the review.
- **Level adjustment.** For A2, use fewer immediate corrections, more explicit ones, focused on high-frequency basics (past simple, 3rd-person -s, question formation, core vocabulary). For B1, add collocations, articles, present perfect vs past simple, and recasts for known forms.

## Q5. Practical techniques of trained teachers (CELTA/DELTA-style): finger correction, reformulation, delayed error slots, "hot vs cold" correction, peer and self-correction, tone and affect

### Takeaway
Practitioner consensus (British Council TeachingEnglish; Ellis 2009's teacher-development guidelines) runs as follows:
- Decide by the aim of the activity: correct on the spot for accuracy work, delay for fluency work.
- Give the learner the first chance to self-correct (gesture, echo, prompt), then a peer, then the teacher.
- Reformulate rather than interrupt.
- Keep correction brief and friendly.
- Revisit errors in a delayed "error slot".

This is consensus and craft knowledge. Its combined effectiveness has not been tested experimentally.

### Inferences (mapping classroom techniques to a voice-only AI)
- **Finger correction becomes spoken segmentation plus stress.** For example: "Say it again, but think about the second word…", or repeat the utterance up to the error and stop ("Yesterday I…").
- **Gestures (e.g., pointing backwards for past tense) become prosody and brief clues.** Use emphatic stress on the problem word, a short pause, a rising "hmm?", or a one-word clue ("Past!").
- **The error slot / "cold" correction becomes an end-of-conversation "language review".** Deliver it by voice plus an in-app text card with a before→after pair and one practice item. Showing the correction in text adds reinforcement and matters for grammar, which learners often miss when it comes as a recast.
- **Peer correction** is not applicable. Its function (learners noticing errors themselves) can be approximated with self-review: "Listen to your sentence — can you spot one thing to change?" Play-back of the learner's own audio is possible in the app.
- **Self-correction first.** Follow Ellis's implicit-to-explicit ladder: prompt, then clue, then explicit correction.
- **Tone.**
  - Respond to the meaning first ("Oh nice!"), then give the form in one short line, then go straight back to the topic.
  - Never use "wrong" or "no".
  - Use humour sparingly.
  - Praise successful self-repair briefly.
  - Warmth and brevity are also practitioner consensus rather than tested variables.

## Q6. Corrective feedback in computer-mediated, ASR-based and AI settings; risks of false corrections; pronunciation specifics

### Takeaway
Automated pronunciation feedback works:
- Pronunciation instruction with feedback shows d ≈ 0.80–0.89 overall, with larger effects when feedback is included (Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015).
- ASR-based training is moderately effective overall, with a large effect on segmentals, a small effect on suprasegmentals, and a need for sustained use (Ngo, Chen & Lai 2024).

However, **automatic error detection in spontaneous learner speech is fragile**. Spoken grammatical error correction (ASR → disfluency removal → grammar correction) suffers from ASR errors on accented, disfluent speech, and these errors flow through the pipeline (Speak & Improve 2025 work). Older computer-assisted pronunciation training (CAPT) research explicitly warns that erroneous feedback can lead to "learning breakdowns". LLM chatbots show emotional benefits, such as lower anxiety, but mixed or limited grammar gains. I found no rigorous study that measures the motivational cost of false AI corrections. The design principle has to be **precision over recall**: missing an error is better than inventing one.

- Phonological feedback is usually recognised as correction (Mackey et al. 2000), so short pronunciation recasts are viable. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-learners-perceive-interactional-feedback/922512A557AA4C89FF55BDF53B01A431)

### Inferences (false-correction guardrails and pronunciation policy)
All of the following are design proposals derived from the findings above.

1. **Precision over recall.** A false correction teaches a wrong form, erodes trust in the tutor, and may raise anxiety, which Sheen (2008) links to learners gaining less from CF. A missed error costs little because errors recur, and a later occurrence can be caught.
   - Set detection thresholds so that corrections are **rarely wrong**, even if many errors go unmentioned.
   - Audit this in production: human-annotate a sample of flagged errors per release and track the **false-correction rate** as a key product metric.
2. **Confidence-gated moves.**
   - *High ASR confidence on the relevant words, and the error is unambiguous:* normal CF.
   - *Medium confidence:* a **confirmation-check recast**, e.g. "Just checking — you *went* to the park yesterday?" If the learner really said "go", this works as a recast. If the ASR misheard, it is a harmless confirmation.
   - *Low confidence:* no correction. Use a genuine clarification request only if the meaning itself is unclear ("Sorry, I didn't catch that — could you say it again?").
3. **Know which error classes the ASR is most likely to get wrong.** These are acoustically weak or easily "normalised" elements:
   - articles (a/the/zero);
   - 3rd-person -s;
   - past -ed;
   - plural -s;
   - short auxiliaries (do/does/have);
   - prepositions.

   Many of these are also typical Russian-L1 error areas, especially articles (unverified; see Q4 Gaps). ASR language models can **hallucinate** a missing article (causing a missed error) or **drop** a spoken one (causing a false error). For these classes:
   - require higher confidence;
   - prefer confirming in the delayed review, after re-transcribing the stored audio with a stronger model;
   - phrase uncertain items with a hedge: "If I heard you right…".
4. **Never correct pronunciation from word-level ASR mismatch alone.** A recognition error on accented speech is not a mispronunciation.
   - Use phone-level scoring with conservative thresholds, and target only a small set of **intelligibility-critical segmentals** for Russian speakers (e.g., θ/ð, w/v, short/long vowels in minimal pairs, final voicing contrasts).
   - Do not correct suprasegmentals (intonation, rhythm) from automatic scores. ASR shows only small effects there (Ngo et al. 2024), and scoring is unreliable. Model them instead ("Listen: I'd LOVE to"), without judging.
5. **Keep LLM corrections minimal.** The model that turns detected errors into feedback should be instructed to:
   - correct genuine errors only, with the **minimal edit**;
   - not rewrite for style or native-like phrasing;
   - not "fix" acceptable variants.

   Over-correction by LLM grammar correctors is a known issue; see Gaps.
6. **Let the learner push back.** Tell the learner the AI can mishear ("I can sometimes mishear — tell me if I got it wrong"), and accept "That's not what I said" gracefully, without arguing. Log contested corrections as likely false positives.
7. **Multimodal reinforcement.** After a spoken correction, show the before→after pair as text in the app. Grammar recasts are often missed as correction (Mackey et al. 2000), and text makes the fix unambiguous. It also lets the learner spot a mis-transcription: "I didn't say that".

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the error rates of current ASR systems (Whisper-class and speech-to-speech models) on Russian-accented L2 English, or their accent-related biases. The search budget was exhausted.
- I did not retrieve precision or false-rejection rates of state-of-the-art mispronunciation detection on L2 English.
- **LLM over-correction** was not retrieved. [RECALLED – VERIFY] Fang et al. (2023), "Is ChatGPT a highly fluent grammatical error correction system?", and related work found that ChatGPT-style models tend to over-correct and make fluency edits beyond the minimal correction.

- The Neri/Cucchiarini design choices (false-rejection minimisation, error limits per utterance) are recalled and should be verified in Cucchiarini et al. (2009) and Neri et al. (2008, *ReCALL*).

## Q7. Concrete example phrasings for each feedback type (spoken English tutor style)

### Takeaway
Effective spoken CF moves share six features:
1. They respond to the meaning first.
2. They change **one** thing.
3. They **stress** the corrected form.
4. They use **declarative** intonation for recasts.
5. They take one breath or less.
6. They go straight back to the conversation.

When the learner cannot self-correct, feedback escalates from implicit to explicit. The phrasings below are **design proposals**. Their features come from Loewen & Philp (2006), Lyster & Ranta's (1997) definitions, British Council technique lists, and Ellis's (2009) implicit-to-explicit guidance. They still need A/B testing with Russian-speaking adults.

### Cited Findings
- Recast features that predict uptake: stress, declarative intonation, one change, multiple feedback moves. Features that predict later test accuracy: interrogative intonation, shortened length, one change. — [Loewen & Philp 2006 (Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2006.00465.x)
- On-the-spot techniques: give the correct answer; echo the learner but correct the language; signal where the problem was so learners self-correct. — [TeachingEnglish: On-the-spot correction](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/n-p/spot-correction)
- Six CF types and prompts vs recasts. — [Lyster & Ranta 1997 (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ539354); [Lyster, Saito & Sato 2013 (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1004287)
- Elicitation had the highest repair rate. — [Lyster & Ranta 1997 via ERIC-hosted study](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1134390.pdf)
- Grammar recasts are often not recognised as correction; pronunciation and vocabulary recasts are. — [Mackey, Gass & McDonough 2000](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-learners-perceive-interactional-feedback/922512A557AA4C89FF55BDF53B01A431)

### Inferences (phrasing library: proposals; CAPS = spoken stress)
The error examples are plausible for A2–B1 Russian speakers. They are illustrative, not frequency-verified.

**1. Conversational recast** (implicit; default in free talk for vocabulary, pronunciation, and grammar the learner already knows at B1)
- L: "Yesterday I go to the park with my dog." → T: "Oh, you WENT to the park — lovely. Was it busy?"
- L: "I am agree with you." → T: "You AGREE — me too! Why do you think so?"
- L: "He has two childs." → T: "Two CHILDREN — how old are they?"
  - One change only. If the sentence had a second error, it goes to the review log.
- L: "I did a mistake at work." → T: "Ah, you MADE a mistake — happens to everyone. What happened?"
- Pronunciation. L: "I sink it's a good idea." → T: "You THINK so? Me too."
  - Lengthen the /θ/ slightly.

**2. Didactic (salient) recast** (for grammar targets at B1; the corrected form stands alone before the conversation continues)
- T: "WENT. Yesterday I went to the park." (short pause) "So — was it busy?"

**3. Explicit correction** (new or unknown forms, A2 learners, or after prompts fail; always short)
- T: "Quick fix: not 'I am agree' — just 'I agree'. 'Agree' is a verb. So, why do you agree?"
- T: "For yesterday we say 'went', not 'go'. Yesterday I went to the park. Your turn?"
  - This is explicit correction plus a light elicitation.

**4. Metalinguistic clue** (prompt; the learner probably knows the rule)
- "Yesterday… so which tense?"
- "Check the verb — it's in the past."
- "Remember: 'people' is already plural."
- "Information — can we count it?"

**5. Elicitation** (prompt; highest repair rate in Lyster & Ranta)
- Completion: "Yesterday I…?" (pause, let the learner finish)
- Question: "How do we say 'go' in the past?"
- Reformulation request: "Can you say that again, in the past?"

**6. Repetition** (prompt; echo up to the error with rising, curious, friendly intonation)
- "Yesterday I GO…?"
- Use sparingly. It needs good TTS prosody and can sound mocking if overused.

**7. Clarification request** (genuine only: when meaning or audio is unclear, never a fake "Sorry?")
- "Sorry, I didn't quite catch that — could you say it again?"
- "Do you mean you *went* there, or you *want to go* there?"
  - This checks the meaning and gives the learner a model of the correct form.

**8. Confirmation-check recast** (for medium ASR confidence; see Q6)
- "Just checking I heard you right — you've lived here SINCE 2019?"

**9. Escalation sequence** (one error, at most 3 moves, then drop it)
1. "Yesterday I…?"
2. "It's yesterday — past tense."
3. "It's 'went'. Yesterday I went. Say it with me?"

After a successful repair: "Yes — went. Perfect." Then go straight back to the topic.

**10. Praise for self-repair** (brief)
- "Exactly!"
- "Yes — nice fix."
- Do not over-praise; a long compliment interrupts the flow as much as a correction does.

**11. Pronunciation (explicit, physical)**
- "Let's try 'th': tongue between your teeth, blow gently — th… think. Now you."
- "Listen: SHIP — short. SHEEP — long. Say 'ship'."
- Stress: "deVELop — not DEvelop. Listen: de-VEL-op."
- Only when the word would be misunderstood or is a session target. Leave accent features that don't affect understanding alone.

**12. Delayed review** ("cold" correction; end of conversation, voice plus text card)
- "Great chat — you spoke for six minutes! Three things to polish:
  1. We say 'I agree', not 'I am agree'.
  2. For yesterday: 'went', not 'go'.
  3. 'Two children', not 'childs'.
  
  Let's practise one: say 'Yesterday I went to…' and finish the sentence."
- Hedged item (low confidence): "I think I heard 'a informations' — if so, remember: 'information', no 'a', no 's'. If I misheard, just skip this one."

**13. Negotiating the correction contract** (onboarding and periodic check-ins)
- "How should I correct you? One: only important mistakes while we talk, and a summary at the end. Two: more corrections as we go. Three: no corrections during the chat — only at the end."
- Mid-session: "Am I correcting too much, or too little?"
- Learner commands to honour: "Correct me more." / "Just let me talk."

**14. Anxiety-sensitive responses**
- L: "Sorry, my English is so bad…" → T: "Your English is good — I understood you perfectly. Let's keep going."
- Then pause immediate CF for several turns.

**15. Avoid**
- "No." / "Wrong." / "That's incorrect." / "You made a mistake."
- Several fixes in one move.
- Grammar lectures of more than one sentence during the conversation.
- Correcting content or opinions.
- Fake "Sorry?" when the AI understood.
- Correcting acceptable variants ("I've got" vs "I have"; British vs American).
- Arguing when the learner says the AI misheard.

**Proposed metrics for A/B tests** (design)
- Uptake and repair rate by CF type.
- Accuracy on the same structure in later free speech; this matches Lyster & Saito's strongest outcome measure.
- False-correction rate from human audits.
- Learner-rated "amount of correction" (too little / right / too much).
- Session length and continuation.
- Correlation with churn.

### Gaps
- None of these phrasings has been empirically tested with Russian-speaking adults or in a speech-to-speech AI. Their wording, stress realisation (TTS prosody) and frequency need A/B testing.
- Ellis's (2009) original example exchanges for each CF type could not be retrieved to quote. [RECALLED]
- I found no study comparing specific spoken CF wordings (e.g., hedged vs unhedged, praise-first vs direct) for their motivational effect on adult learners.
