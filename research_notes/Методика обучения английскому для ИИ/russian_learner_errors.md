## 1. Grammar: which errors do Russian speakers make, and why (L1 transfer)?

### Takeaway
Articles have the strongest evidence of any Russian-specific grammar problem.
- **Articles:** large corpus studies show that learners whose L1 has no definite article omit articles more. Russian-L1 writers keep making article errors from B1 to B2, while Spanish-L1 writers improve.
- **Other widely reported problems:** tense/aspect (Present Perfect, and the present used for past or future), prepositions and verb government, and "be" errors ("I am agree"). These rest mostly on practitioner sources. REALEC data gives some corpus support for the tense and preposition errors.
- **Everything else in the catalogue** (do-support, dummy *it*/*there*, negative concord, *will* in time clauses, "I want that you…") is well motivated by contrastive analysis but was not sourced independently in this session.

### Cited Findings

**Articles**
- [E] Öksüz, Alexopoulou, Derkach & Tsimpli (2025, *Second Language Research*) analysed article accuracy in about 0.5 million EFCAMDAT writings by learners with 11 typologically diverse L1s. Whether the L1 has a definite article predicts article omission in L2 English, for both *the* and *a*. — [Sage (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1177/02676583251395876); [Cambridge repository](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/1d3e75f2-5eec-4435-b3db-811b8123814d)
- [E] A Cambridge Learner Corpus study looked at 200 exam scripts: 100 by Spanish-L1 writers and 100 by Russian- and Ukrainian-L1 writers, split evenly between B1 and B2.
  - Spanish speakers "show an increase in correct article use with proficiency". In contrast, "speakers of Russian, an article-less language, continue to make article errors".
  - The study also tested whether the overuse of *the* with specific indefinites, found in experiments, appears in corpus data.
  - Published as a chapter in *Learner Corpus Research Meets Second Language Acquisition* (CUP), pp. 10–38. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/learner-corpus-research-meets-second-language-acquisition/article-use-in-russian-and-spanish-learner-writing-at-cefr-b1-and-b2-levels-effects-of-proficiency-native-language-and-specificity/84A1B187650C0744855110FEB13AC247); [Illinois Experts record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/article-use-in-russian-and-spanish-learner-writing-at-cefr-b1-and/)
- [E] An EFCAMDAT analysis of 660 scripts "confirms the lower overall accuracy of learners with no-article L1s". Learners "tend to associate specificity with *a*". As a result they omit the article with non-specific indefinite singulars and overuse *a* with specific indefinite mass nouns. — [SSLA: "Definite and indefinite article accuracy in learner English: A multifactorial analysis"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/definite-and-indefinite-article-accuracy-in-learner-english-a-multifactorial-analysis/FF80FBB18B93015F568E374921498E9E) *(attrib. uncertain)*
- [E] Ionin, Ko & Wexler (2004) found that L2 English speakers whose L1 has no articles (Russian, Korean) switch between using *the/a* for definiteness and for specificity. The predicted errors are overuse of *the* with specific indefinites and overuse of *a* with non-specific definites. — [Ionin et al., GASLA proceedings paper](http://www.lingref.com/cpp/gasla/9/paper1626.pdf); [Ionin (2003), MIT dissertation](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/7963)
- [E] In earlier elicitation studies, article omission by L1 Russian and Korean speakers "was low and rarely exceeded 15%". Learners from article-less L1s omit *the* more often than *a*, and learners from very different L1s overgeneralise *the*. — ["Corpus-based evidence of article omissions by Russian speaking English learners: A new pedagogical list" (Humanities Commons)](https://hcommons.org/deposits/item/hc:45841/); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342960793_Corpus-based_evidence_of_article_omissions_by_Russian_speaking_English_learners_A_new_pedagogical_list) *(attrib. uncertain)*

**Tense and aspect**
- [P] Russian learners "mistakenly use the past simple tense instead of the present perfect". Russian has three tenses plus perfective/imperfective aspect, and "perfect and progressive tenses, as understood in English, do not exist in Russian". — [TALK Schools: common grammar errors](https://blog.talk.edu/learn-english/common-grammar-errors-russian-speakers/); [TALK Schools: grammar issues](https://blog.talk.edu/grammar/english-grammar-issues-for-russian-esl-students/)
- [P] "A typical English learner from Russia almost exclusively uses" simple and continuous forms, "with the addition of perfect tense only possible for upper-level students". — [ITTT TEFL blog](https://www.teflcourse.net/blog/problems-for-english-learners-in-russia-ittt-tefl-blog/)
- [E, small-scale] A pre/post-test study compared early and late Russian–English bilinguals on Past Simple vs Present Perfect accuracy. Late bilinguals improved significantly more. — [Int. J. of English Language & Literature Studies](https://archive.aessweb.com/index.php/5019/article/view/5629)
- [E] Tense errors are among the most frequent patterns in L1-interference data derived from REALEC. One example: past and future events described in the present tense when interpreting graphs (IELTS-type Task 1). "Tense semantics" is one of the word-level interference types that fine-tuned models detect best. — [RILEC, LREC 2026 (ACL Anthology)](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.848/); [arXiv 2603.07366](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07366) *(attrib. of the graph example uncertain)*

**"Be"; agree/depend/mean; countability; infinitives**
- [P] "I am agree" is described as "a classic of the genre" among Russian speakers' mistakes (correct: "I agree"). "It depends from" is listed among the most common mistakes (correct: "depends on"). — [Lacy Edney: 10 common mistakes](https://www.lacyedney.com/single-post/10-common-mistakes-russian-speakers-make-in-english); [ForumDaily: 40 most common mistakes](https://www.forumdaily.com/en/sorok-samyx-rasprostranennyx-oshibok-v-anglijskom-yazyke-kotorye-delayut-russkogovoryashhie/) *(attrib. between these two uncertain)*
- [P] The verb BE and the verbs *agree, depend, mean, seem* are presented as among the hardest grammar topics for Russian speakers. — [TES: Common mistakes of Russian speakers in English (mini-course)](https://www.tes.com/en-us/teaching-resource/common-mistakes-of-russian-speakers-in-english-mini-course-pdf-12995916); [TES extract](https://www.tes.com/en-us/teaching-resource/common-mistakes-of-russian-speakers-in-english-extract-12994753)
- [P] Russian speakers treat English uncountable nouns as countable ("informations"). — [Student Languages: 50+ examples](https://studentlanguages.com/common-mistakes-by-russians/) *(attrib. uncertain; this came from a summary of several practitioner pages)*
- [P] Learners say "I want learn" for "I want to learn", from «Я хочу учиться». — [Study.ru: Typical mistakes of Russian learners of English](https://www.study.ru/article/grammar/typical-mistakes-of-russian-learners-of-english) *(attrib. uncertain)*

**Prepositions**
- [E] *For* is the second most frequently misused preposition in REALEC texts. — [Vinogradova & Login (2017), HSE working paper](https://wp.hse.ru/data/2017/12/13/1160229025/60LNG2017.pdf) *(attrib. uncertain)*
- [E/analytical] English and Russian prepositions cover different ranges of meaning, so Russian speakers face predictable "negative interference zones". The authors' predictions were confirmed against real learners' errors. — [Translating prepositions from Russian legal texts into English: interference zones (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356396109_Translating_Prepositions_from_Russian_Legal_Texts_Into_English_An_Analysis_of_the_Corresponding_Interference_Zones_for_Teaching_Purposes)
- [P] A practitioner collection of Russian speakers' preposition errors exists; its content could not be retrieved. — [OnlineTeachersUK: prepositions](https://onlineteachersuk.com/russian-mistakes-in-english-prepositions/)

**Other inferences**
- **Speech vs writing:**
  - In spoken A2–B1 English, the most *audible* and most *meaning-affecting* grammar errors are probably question formation (G08, G12), missing or extra "be" (G07, G21, G22), tense/time reference (G06, G28, G31, G33), *it/there* (G23, G24) and he/she for objects (G26).

## 3. Pronunciation: which features are typical, and which most affect intelligibility?

### Takeaway
Typical Russian features are:
- **Vowels:** Russian has about six vowel phonemes and no length contrast.
- **Consonants:** final devoicing; /w/→[v], /h/→[x], /θ ð/→[s z]/[t d]/[f]; palatalisation.
- **Stress and intonation:** transferred cognate stress, few weak forms, and Russian intonation contours.

For **intelligibility**:
- The Lingua Franca Core puts vowel-length contrasts and nuclear stress in the "core" and /θ ð/ outside it.
- Experimental work shows Russian listeners separate /iː/ and /ɪ/ by length alone, ignoring vowel quality.
- Russian intonation laid over English speech is judged more negative by English listeners. It is a politeness risk more than an intelligibility risk.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Kondaurova & Francis (J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 2008): Russian listeners showed "no evidence of attending to vowel quality" when identifying English /i/ vs /ɪ/, and relied almost entirely on duration. English listeners relied mainly on spectral quality. The authors explain this by both English vowels assimilating to the single Russian /i/ category, plus Russian experience with sub-phonemic duration differences. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2737250/); [related paper on Russian listeners (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260492733_Perception_of_the_English_tenselax_vowel_contrast_by_native_speakers_of_Russian)
- [E] Russian final devoicing is "incomplete neutralization" (Journal of Phonetics, 2010). Native Russians who knew English kept a voicing difference in Russian words through vowel duration and voicing into closure/frication, and higher English proficiency correlated with larger vowel-duration differences. — [ScienceDirect: "Phonological neutralization by native and non-native speakers: The case of Russian final devoicing"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0095447010000434)
- [P] Practitioner descriptions of Russian-accented English:
  - Russian speakers "tend to devoice voiced consonants at the end of words, so 'bag' might sound like 'back'".
  - Russian has /v/ but not /w/, "so words like west and vest are often pronounced similarly".
  - The Russian vowel system "has fewer distinctions than English"; "even advanced speakers may struggle".
  - Russian has a system of palatalised (soft) and non-palatalised (hard) consonants.
  - English is stress-timed and Russian is claimed to be "more syllable-timed". This last claim is contested; see Inferences.
  - Sources: [Laura Patsko: English with a Russian accent](https://laurapatsko.wordpress.com/2019/02/20/english-with-a-russian-accent/); [Accentify: 6 common mistakes](https://www.accentify.co.uk/blog/6-common-english-pronunciation-mistakes-russian-speakers-make); [Talkpal](https://talkpal.ai/mastering-the-russian-accent-speaking-english-tips-and-tricks-revealed/) *(attrib. across these pages uncertain)*
- [E] Holden & Hogan (1993, *Language and Speech* 36(1): 67–88) swapped English and Russian intonation while keeping other phonetic factors constant.
  - English listeners were "more sensitive to foreign intonation".
  - The biggest difference was in judgements of Negative emotions and attitudes: Russian intonation was "consistently rated as more Negative".
  - Source: [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002383099303600104)
- [P/E] "Russian English learners tend to ask questions with falling instead of rising intonation which is perceived as impolite by English native speakers." — [European Journal of Natural History: "English intonation and the Russian learner"](https://world-science.ru/en/article/view?id=33348); [Perception of EFL intonation by Chinese and Russian speakers (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323944054_PERCEPTION_OF_EFL_INTONATION_IN_CONTEXT_BY_THE_SPEAKERS_OF_TONE_CHINESE_AND_INTONATION_RUSSIAN_LANGUAGES) *(attrib. uncertain)*
- [P] Intonational interference makes Russian speakers' English sound "unnatural, flat, or robotic". — [LinguaLinkDC: English pronunciation for Russian speakers](https://www.lingualinkdc.net/blog/englishrussian) *(attrib. uncertain)*
- [Theory/E] Jenkins' Lingua Franca Core (LFC) aims at intelligibility, not a native accent.
  - It includes all consonants **except /θ, ð/**.
  - It treats **vowel length** as core, especially before final consonants.
  - It treats **nuclear stress** placement as core.
  - Sources: [Lingua Franca Core (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_Franca_Core); [ELF Pronunciation blog](https://elfpron.wordpress.com/2013/11/21/what-is-the-lfc/); [IH Journal: LFC on CELTA/DELTA](http://ihjournal.com/the-impact-of-jenkins%E2%80%99-lingua-franca-core-on-the-teaching-of-pronunciation-on-celta-and-delta-courses-by-eleanor-spicer)

- **Suggested intelligibility ranking for Russian speakers.** This is an inference from the LFC, Kondaurova & Francis, and minimal-pair reasoning:
  1. Vowel contrasts: /ɪ–iː/, /æ–e/, /ɜː/, /əʊ–ɒ/ (P01, P08–P10). They carry grammatical meaning (man/men); vowel length is LFC core; Russians rely on duration only.
  2. Final voicing and the vowel length before it (P02, P17). LFC core, and it affects the -s and -ed endings.
  3. Word stress on international words and cognates (P19). Very frequent for Russians because of shared vocabulary.
  4. /w/→[v] (P03): an LFC core consonant in very frequent words (we, was, what, where, will, would, work).
  5. Nuclear/contrastive stress and weak forms, e.g. can/can't (P20, P21).
  6. Aspiration (P14).
  7. Low intelligibility impact but noticeable accent: /θ ð/, [x], trilled r, dark l, palatalisation (P05–P07, P16).
- Intonation (P04, P22) matters little for word recognition, but the Holden & Hogan result suggests it strongly shapes how a speaker's attitude is perceived. **Frame it under pragmatics** ("sounding friendly"), not as an "error".
- **Rhythm conflict:** practitioner pages call Russian "more syllable-timed". Standard rhythm typology (from memory; not verified here) usually groups Russian with stress-timed languages because of its strong unstressed-vowel reduction. The practical problem is more likely the missing weak forms of English function words (P20) than rhythm class. Flag this before putting "syllable-timed" into a prompt.
- **Correction cue for P01:** Russians already exaggerate *length*, so a tutor should cue *quality*: "ship: short, relaxed, mouth a bit more open". A cue of just "longer" is not enough. *(Inference from Kondaurova & Francis)*

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures which Russian-accent features most reduce intelligibility of *English* for native or ELF listeners. The ranking above is inferred.
- The LFC's inclusion of aspiration and Field's (2005, *TESOL Quarterly*) result that misplaced lexical stress harms intelligibility come from the researcher's memory and were not verified here. The same applies to Munro & Derwing's (2006, *System*) functional-load finding.
- The findings of the JASA-EL vowel-assimilation study, the Int. J. Audiology phoneme-confusion study and the Crosby thesis were not retrieved.

## 4. Pragmatics: directness, politeness, negative questions, small talk

### Takeaway
Cross-cultural research (Larina; Iliadi on refusals) finds Russian communicative norms more direct and less mitigated than British and American ones, so faithful transfer can sound rude. This covers bare imperatives with «пожалуйста», "I want…" requests, blunt refusals, "must" in advice and falling-tone questions. Negative questions work differently: Russian да can confirm a negative proposition. The individual speech-act rows are practitioner or contrastive, with no frequency data.

### Cited Findings
- [E/theory] Larina (2008), "Directness, imposition and politeness in English and Russian", *Cambridge ESOL Research Notes* 33: 33–38, compares directness, imposition and politeness in the two languages and their effects on intercultural communication. — [Research Notes 33 (PDF)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/23152-research-notes-33.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/288965523_Directness_imposition_and_politeness_in_English_and_Russian)
- [P, secondary] Larina describes the difference as **distance** vs **closeness**:
  - Anglo cultures value privacy and autonomy; politeness means not intruding.
  - Russian culture values solidarity and involvement.
  - Source: [Polyglottist Language Academy blog](https://www.polyglottistlanguageacademy.com/language-culture-travelling-blog/2026/3/16/why-russians-value-depth-over-politeness) *(secondary source; attrib. uncertain)*
- [E] In refusals, "the Russians use more direct strategies than the British and are more taciturn and laconic". The British "do more face-work to mitigate their refusal", use negative and positive politeness strategies "with higher regularity" and "are more voluble". — [Iliadi, "Refusal strategies in English and Russian", RUDN J. of Language Studies, Semiotics and Semantics](https://journals.rudn.ru/semiotics-semantics/article/view/17014)
- [P] Russian да/нет interact with negative questions in a way that is the "mirror image" of English. ## 5. Frequency and persistence evidence (learner corpora; A2–B1 patterns)

### Takeaway
- **Articles** have the only solid quantitative evidence: EFCAMDAT at scale and CLC B1→B2.
- **REALEC** (HSE) is the main Russian-L1 error-annotated corpus, and REALEC/RILEC show tense errors, preposition errors and transliteration as frequent interference types. Per-category percentages could not be retrieved.
- **All evidence is written** and mostly from university-level writers. Frequencies in A2–B1 *speech* have to be inferred.

### Cited Findings
- [E] REALEC (Russian Error-Annotated Learner English Corpus) was built from 2012 at the School of Linguistics, HSE Moscow. The first published version (Kuzmenko & Kutuzov, 2014) had about 225,000 word tokens from 794 essays. — [ACL Anthology W14-3507](https://aclanthology.org/W14-3507.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Russian-Error-Annotated-Learner-English-Corpus:-a-Kuzmenko-Kutuzov/166b41ccc1f4e4dedd05e24d137bd960559360e9); [HSE publication page](https://publications.hse.ru/en/chapters/140804312)
- [E] A later version, including 2020 exam essays, has 18,710 texts and 4,833,749 tokens. The 2020 folder was annotated by a neural network into 6 error types. — [REALEC: About](https://realec.org/about/); [web-corpora.net REALEC page](http://web-corpora.net/realec/) *(attrib. between these uncertain)*
- [E] By 2017 REALEC held more than 3,500 texts written by Bachelor students preparing for the English exam. Expert annotators mark the error span, assign one or more tags and supply a correction. The tag groups are spelling, punctuation, grammar, vocabulary and discourse. — [Vinogradova & Login (2017), HSE WP](https://wp.hse.ru/data/2017/12/13/1160229025/60LNG2017.pdf); [REALEC tagging manual](https://realec.org/REALEC_annotation_manual.pdf)
- [E] REALEC essays are written for IELTS-like tasks. RILEC (LREC 2026) is built on REALEC and has 18,000+ sentences with interference errors: expert-annotated plus rule-based and neural synthetic examples. — [RILEC (ACL Anthology)](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.848/); [LREC 2026 page](https://lrec.elra.info/lrec2026-main-848)
- [E] Tense errors are among the most frequent interference patterns (present used for past/future in graph descriptions), and *for* is the second most misused preposition. — [RILEC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07366); [Vinogradova & Login](https://wp.hse.ru/data/2017/12/13/1160229025/60LNG2017.pdf) *(attrib. uncertain)*
- [E] Articles:
  - The availability of a definite article in the L1 predicts omission (about 0.5M EFCAMDAT texts, 11 L1s). — [Öksüz et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/02676583251395876)
  - Russian/Ukrainian-L1 writers show no improvement in article accuracy from B1 to B2 in the CLC, unlike Spanish-L1 writers. — [CLC chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/learner-corpus-research-meets-second-language-acquisition/article-use-in-russian-and-spanish-learner-writing-at-cefr-b1-and-b2-levels-effects-of-proficiency-native-language-and-specificity/84A1B187650C0744855110FEB13AC247)
  - Omission rates in elicitation tasks "rarely exceeded 15%". — [hcommons](https://hcommons.org/deposits/item/hc:45841/) *(attrib. uncertain)*
- [P] Perfect tenses appear only among upper-level Russian learners. — [ITTT](https://www.teflcourse.net/blog/problems-for-english-learners-in-russia-ittt-tefl-blog/)
- [Background] Proshina's work on "Russian English" as a World English variety separates a mesolectal "Russian English" from "Ruslish", meaning learners' deficient English. It has a chapter on "Linguistic features of Russian English" that could be mined for more features. — [CUP: Russian English](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/russian-english/99E08FD936F9C837727D6C3605614C8B); [Ch. 4: Linguistic features](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/russian-english/linguistic-features-of-russian-english/B4D1AE3FA48E942668EEDC29584A724F); [Rivlina, "Russian English and what it is not" (RJL)](https://journals.rudn.ru/linguistics/article/view/24404)

### Inferences
- **Estimated ranking of errors in A2–B1 speech.** This is a judgement combining the corpus evidence with the contrastive rows; it is *not* a measured ranking.

  **Grammar and vocabulary:**
  1. Articles (G01–G04): most frequent and most persistent, with [E] evidence.
  2. Tense/time reference (G05, G06, G28, G31, G33): [E] REALEC plus [P].
  3. Prepositions and verb government (G09, G61–G74): [E] REALEC plus [P].
  4. Question formation and do-support (G08, G12, G14).
  5. Be-errors (G07, G21, G22).
  6. *it/there* existentials (G23, G24).
  7. Lexical transfer (false friends, calques, make/do; V/C rows): [E] RILEC transliteration.
  8. Countability and number (G04, G10, G54, G55).
  9. Adverb and word order (G53).
  10. Gender pronouns for objects (G26).

  **Pronunciation:** vowels (P01, P08–P10), final devoicing (P02), cognate stress (P19), /w/ (P03), intonation (P04, P22).

- **Persistence:** articles are the clearest fossilisation risk ([E] CLC). Perfect aspect, prepositions, vowel quality and intonation are described by practitioners as persisting in advanced speakers. Copula and do-support errors are expected to fade by B1 but can resurface under fluency pressure. *(Inference)*
- **Level mapping** *(inference)*:
  - A2 is dominated by clause-building errors: missing "be" or "I am + verb", no do-support, no *it/there*, negation with "not", he/she for things, "Me too" for "Me neither", «пожалуйста» errors.
  - B1 is dominated by tense-aspect choices (PP, for/since, future forms, time clauses), reported speech, conditionals, gerund/infinitive, indirect questions, collocations and false friends.
- **REALEC writers are university students** doing IELTS-type tasks, likely B1+. REALEC frequencies will therefore *under-represent* basic morphosyntax errors typical of A2 app users and *over-represent* academic-writing errors (graph description, punctuation). *(Inference)*

- No spoken error-annotated corpus of Russian-L1 English was found.
- No data was found on A2-level Russian learners specifically. EFCAMDAT has level information and could supply it.

## 6. Prioritisation for an A2–B1 voice tutor (synthesis for the prompt)

### Takeaway
Correct first whatever is **frequent, audible and changes meaning or politeness**:
- **Grammar:** question formation, "be" errors, time reference, *it/there*, negation and short answers.
- **Words:** key false friends and calques.
- **Pronunciation:** the vowel and final-voicing contrasts.
- **Pragmatics:** request, repair and refusal routines.

Treat articles and accent-only features (/θ ð/, [x], palatalisation) mostly through recasts and focused practice, not constant interruption. The evidence for this ordering is partly empirical (LFC, CLC persistence, Holden & Hogan) and partly inference.

### Cited Findings
- [Theory/E] LFC priorities: /θ ð/ non-core; vowel length core; nuclear stress core. — [ELF Pronunciation blog](https://elfpron.wordpress.com/2013/11/21/what-is-the-lfc/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_Franca_Core)
- [E] Russian learners rely on duration, not quality, for /iː/–/ɪ/. — [Kondaurova & Francis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2737250/)
- [E] Russian intonation is rated more negative by English listeners. — [Holden & Hogan 1993](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002383099303600104)
- [E] Russian-L1 article errors persist from B1 to B2. — [CLC chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/learner-corpus-research-meets-second-language-acquisition/article-use-in-russian-and-spanish-learner-writing-at-cefr-b1-and-b2-levels-effects-of-proficiency-native-language-and-specificity/84A1B187650C0744855110FEB13AC247)
- [E, indirect] Comprehensibility depends on all domains (pronunciation, lexis, grammar), while accentedness is mainly segmental. — [L2 Russian speech study](https://www.academia.edu/52870298/The_Intelligibility_and_Comprehensibility_of_Learner_Speech_in_Russian_A_Study_in_the_Salience_of_Pronunciation_Lexicon_Grammar_and_Syntax)

### Inferences
- **Tier 1: correct actively (A2–B1).**
  - Grammar: G07, G08, G12, G15–G18, G21–G24, G26, G28, G31, G33, G39, G43, G44, G53, G58, G59.
  - Prepositions: G61–G64, G66, G68, G70.
  - Vocabulary: V01, V05, V13, C01–C03, C07.
  - Pronunciation: P01–P03, P08, P19.
  - Pragmatics: PR01, PR03–PR07.
- **Tier 2: correct when it is the lesson focus or it recurs (mostly B1).**
  - Grammar: G05, G06, G14, G29, G30, G34–G37, G40–G42, G45–G52, G54–G56, G60, G65, G67, G69, G71–G74.
  - Vocabulary: V02–V04, V07–V09, V14–V22, C04–C13.
  - Pronunciation: P09–P15, P17, P18, P20–P22.
  - Pragmatics: PR02, PR08–PR12.
- **Tier 3: tolerate, or recast without comment.**
  - Grammar: G13, G20, G27, G32, G38, G57.
  - Vocabulary: V06, V10–V12, V23–V26.
  - Pronunciation: P05–P07, P16.
- **Articles:** recast in open conversation. Correct explicitly only in high-frequency frames: "I'm **a/an** + job", "**the** + superlative", "**a** + singular count noun after *have / there is*". Teach the rule as "first mention → a; we both know which → the". Russian speakers otherwise read *the* as "a specific one I'm thinking of" (Ionin et al.).
- **Explanations:** a one-line L1 contrast in Russian is likely to work well for transfer errors, for example: «по-русски "согласен" — прилагательное, а agree — глагол: I agree». Developmental errors (G60) should *not* be blamed on Russian.
- **Vowel cues:** cue vowel quality, not just length (P01). Cue vowel length before voiced final consonants rather than "voicing" alone (P02; LFC).
- **Politeness framing:** frame intonation and pragmatic items as "sounding friendly and polite", not as mistakes. Holden & Hogan show that listeners judge attitude, not correctness.
- **Unverified support for de-prioritising some errors** (from the researcher's memory; verify before citing):
  - Seidlhofer (2004, *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics* 24) lists lexico-grammatical features that rarely cause communication problems in English as a lingua franca. The list covers dropping 3sg -s, using *who* and *which* interchangeably, and omitting or inserting articles. It also covers invariant "isn't it?", redundant prepositions ("discuss about"), heavy use of *do/have/make/put/take*, and "I want that…" clauses. This overlaps strongly with Russian errors (G01–G04, G20, G39, G44, G56, G60) and supports lower *interruption* priority in free talk, **not** ignoring them. Many users will have exam or professional accuracy goals.
- **ASR caveat** (not researched; inference): ASR models with strong language models may "auto-correct" learner speech, for example by inserting missing articles or normalising "he go" or "informations". Error detection may then miss real errors. Accented vowels (ship/sheep, bad/bed) may also be mis-transcribed and produce **false** grammar or vocabulary flags. The tutor should confirm before correcting anything that may be an ASR artefact.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that ranks Russian-L1 English errors by their effect on listener comprehension. The tiers above are a reasoned synthesis.
- Seidlhofer (2004), Field (2005), Munro & Derwing (2006) and the LFC aspiration criterion were not verified in this session.
- The ASR behaviour on Russian-accented learner speech was not researched and needs separate testing with the chosen speech-recognition engine.
