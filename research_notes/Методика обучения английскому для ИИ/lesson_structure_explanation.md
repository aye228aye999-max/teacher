## 1. What lesson staging do Cambridge CELTA/DELTA, British Council, International House and Bell recommend, and what is each stage for?

### Takeaway
Cambridge / British Council training does not prescribe one lesson shape. It assesses functions:
- a clear, communicative context for the language;
- clarification of meaning, form and pronunciation "to an appropriate degree/depth";
- checking of understanding;
- appropriate practice;
- achievement of stated aims.

The frameworks taught (PPP, Test-Teach-Test, Harmer's ESA, Scrivener's ARC, Willis's task cycle) are alternative orderings of the same building blocks: engage/lead-in, context, clarify and check, restricted (controlled) practice, authentic (freer) use, and feedback / language focus.

### Cited Findings
**Cambridge CELTA: official documents [PRACT]**
- The CELTA syllabus has five topic areas:
  1. learners and teachers, and the teaching and learning context;
  2. language analysis and awareness;
  3. language skills (reading, listening, speaking, writing);
  4. planning and resources for different teaching contexts;
  5. developing teaching skills and professionalism.

  Assessment has two components: (1) planning and teaching, and (2) classroom-related written assignments. It is internally assessed and externally moderated by a Cambridge-approved assessor. — [Cambridge English: CELTA Syllabus and assessment guidelines](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/21816-celta-syllabus.pdf)
- Overall assessment aim: candidates should "assess learner needs, and plan and teach lessons which take account of learners' backgrounds, learning preferences and current needs". — [CELTA syllabus](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/21816-celta-syllabus.pdf)
- Criterion **2c** reads "providing clear contexts and a communicative focus for language". Trainers call it the "setting the context" criterion. — [English for Africa: CELTA criterion 2c, Setting the Context](https://www.englishforafrica.net/post/celta-assessment-criteria-explained-criterion-2c-setting-the-context)
- Criterion **2e** covers clarification:
  - older syllabus wording: "focusing on language items in the classroom by clarifying forms, meanings and pronunciation to an appropriate degree";
  - CELTA5 wording: "clarifying relevant aspects of meaning, form and phonology to an appropriate depth". [verify exact current wording]

  Sources: [CELTA syllabus](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/21816-celta-syllabus.pdf). A trainer guide teaches the meaning → form → pronunciation (MFP) sequence: [DC Teacher Training: How to clarify meaning, form and pronunciation step by step](https://www.dcteachertraining.com/post/how-to-clarify-meaning-form-pronunciation-celta)
- CELTA planning guidance: "All plans start with a consideration of the learners, their needs, their learning preferences and their interests". [verify page attribution] — [ELT Concourse: CELTA lesson planning](https://www.eltconcourse.com/training/initial/celta/planning_celta.html)

**Frameworks taught on CELTA-type courses and by the British Council [PRACT]**

*PPP*
- Presentation (context plus clarification), then Practice (controlled, accuracy-focused, often drilling), then Production (freer use). Characterised as deductive and teacher-centred, with accuracy mainly through drilling. — [ResearchGate: PPP vs TBLT, a comparison of two teaching templates](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375642779_PRESENTATION_PRACTICE_PRODUCTION_VS_TASK-BASED_LANGUAGE_TEACHING_A_COMPARISON_OF_TWO_TEACHING_TEMPLATES)
- Harmer's "straight arrow" ESA sequence is "very similar to PPP". — [Barefoot TEFL Teacher](https://www.barefootteflteacher.com/p/what-is-engage-study-activate)
- PPP originated at the dawn of communicative language teaching, not in audiolingualism. — [Anderson 2017, ELT Journal 71(2)](https://academic.oup.com/eltj/article-abstract/71/2/218/2447419)

*Test-Teach-Test (TTT)*
- The lesson starts with a diagnostic "test" activity to find which aspects of the target grammar or vocabulary learners already know. The "teach" phase then covers only what they do not know, followed by a second test. — [British Council TeachingEnglish: Test teach test](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/article/test-teach-test); [DC Teacher Training: TTT framework for CELTA](https://medium.com/@info_24453/test-teach-test-a-framework-for-celta-grammar-and-vocabulary-lessons-5d7bc13fdd4a)
- According to the British Council, TTT "can be particularly useful at intermediate levels and above, where learners may have seen language before, but have specific problems with it, and also in mixed level classes". — [British Council: Test teach test](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/article/test-teach-test)
- The British Council defines a diagnostic as "an activity that a teacher carries out with learners in order to identify problem areas they may have. This data is then used to plan further activities." — [British Council: Diagnostic](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/d-h/diagnostic)
- A 2024 history of TTT exists but was not read: [Jason Anderson (2024)](https://jasonanderson.blog/2024/05/30/on-the-origins-history-and-understanding-of-test-teach-test-in-english-language-teaching/)

*Harmer's ESA*
- Three phases:
  - **Engage** "warms up students and prepares them for learning";
  - **Study** focuses on the new content or form;
  - **Activate** has learners apply it in practical, freer use.
- Three sequences:
  - **Straight arrow**: E→S→A, i.e. PPP-like; predictable and easiest to plan.
  - **Boomerang**: E → Activate 1 → Study → Activate 2. Learners attempt first, study the gaps, then re-use.
  - **Patchwork**: a flexible mix of the three.
- Sources: [Barefoot TEFL Teacher: What is Engage, Study, Activate?](https://www.barefootteflteacher.com/p/what-is-engage-study-activate); [TESOL Pop: using Harmer's ESA model](https://tesolpop.com/tesolpopblogs/2023/4/30/from-linear-to-flexible-using-jeremy-harmers-esa-model-for-lesson-planning)

*Scrivener's ARC (from* Learning Teaching*)*
- Three components:
  - **Authentic use**: meaning, fluency and engagement, e.g. a discussion that includes the target language.
  - **Restricted use**: controlled practice focused on form and accuracy, e.g. gap-fills.
  - **Clarification and focus**: explaining the item's use, form and meaning.
- The components "can appear in any order in the lesson, depending on aims, level and focus". "Students should have the opportunity to participate in all three aspects in every lesson."
- Scrivener offers ARC as a tool for analysing lesson stages by what students get out of them, not as a teaching method.
- Sources: [British Council TeachingEnglish: ARC](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/c/arc); [Sanako: the ARC lesson structure](https://sanako.com/why-is-the-arc-lesson-structure-so-powerful-for-language-teaching)

*Willis's task-based framework (J. Willis,* A Framework for Task-Based Learning*, 1996)*
- **Pre-task:** the teacher introduces the topic, activates prior knowledge, teaches useful words and phrases, and makes sure learners understand the instructions and what outcome they will present.
- **Task cycle:** task, then planning (preparing to report how they did it and what they found), then report to the class.
- **Language focus:** explicit analysis and practice of forms that arose during the cycle.
- Stated purpose: to "create a real purpose for language use and provide a natural context for language study". The conditions for learning are exposure, real use, motivation and focus on form.
- Sources: [J. Willis: A flexible framework for task-based learning (PDF)](https://www3.gobiernodecanarias.org/medusa/proyecto/38010141-0004/wp-content/uploads/sites/245/2018/01/a-flexible-framework-for-task-based-learning.pdf); [D. & J. Willis: Task-based learning](https://www2.vobs.at/ludescher/grammar/task_based_learning.htm)

**International House, Bell, DELTA**
- International House publishes the IH Journal. IH London's teacher-training video series lets trainees watch lessons "stage by stage", with interactive tasks explaining how procedures support learning. No IH-specific staging model could be retrieved. — [IH Journal](https://ihworld.com/ih-journal/); [IH London Teacher Portal: teacher-training videos](https://teacherportal.ihlondon.com/course/ihlondon-teacher-training-videos)

### Inferences
- **The frameworks share six stage functions; only the order differs.**
  - PPP: context → clarify and check → restricted practice → authentic use.
  - TTT / boomerang: engage → authentic attempt (diagnostic) → clarify the gaps → restricted practice → authentic use again.
  - Willis: pre-task input → task → planning → report → language focus.

  The app's flow engine should implement six reusable stage types (lead-in, context, clarify and check, restricted practice, authentic task, feedback / language focus) and assemble them per lesson type. It should not hard-code one sequence.
- **"Appropriate degree/depth" means proportionate clarification, not exhaustive explanation.** This supports short explanation clips plus checking.
- **Make boomerang/TTT the default for this audience.** The target learners "understand but can't speak" and have usually met A2–B1 grammar before, and the British Council recommends TTT for learners who "have seen language before". Start with a short spoken diagnostic task, then play clarification clips only for forms that failed.
- **Give every stage an explicit aim and a machine-checkable exit condition**, for example:
  - context understood;
  - CCQs answered;
  - restricted-practice accuracy above a threshold;
  - task outcome achieved;
  - feedback delivered.

  This lets the app code control transitions.
- **Proposed default 20-minute structure.** This is a design proposal, not an evidence-derived norm. It compresses CELTA-style staging and maximises learner speaking time.

| Time | Stage | Delivery | Stage aim / exit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–1:30 | Lead-in | AI live | Personal, topic-linked question; state the can-do goal; activate schema and relevance |
| 1:30–4:00 | Diagnostic task ("Test" / Activate 1) | AI live | Short speaking task that naturally needs the target language; AI logs attempts at the form; recasts only |
| 4:00–6:30 | Clarify meaning → form → pronunciation, then check | Pre-recorded clip(s) of 30–120 s each, then 2–4 AI CCQs | Understanding confirmed; if a CCQ fails, play an alternative clip (contrast pair / Russian contrast / timeline) |
| 6:30–10:00 | Restricted practice | AI live | Accuracy on the target form; meaningful drills, from controlled to less controlled; immediate correction on the target form |
| 10:00–16:00 | Authentic use / task (Activate 2) | AI live | Fluency and task outcome; errors noted for delayed feedback |
| 16:00–19:00 | Feedback + language focus | AI live | 2–3 reformulations of the learner's own errors; learner repeats; partial task repeat |
| 19:00–20:00 | Close | AI live | Learner self-assesses the can-do; one-line rule + example; schedule recycling |

## 2. PPP vs Test-Teach-Test vs Task-Based Language Teaching (Willis, Ellis, Long): what does the evidence say, and when does each suit adult A2–B1 learners?

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses show TBLT programmes work, but the size of their advantage is contested:
- Bryfonski & McKay (2019): d = 0.93.
- Xuan et al.'s reanalysis: g = 0.61.
- Bryfonski and McKay themselves later co-authored a call for cautious interpretation.

Head-to-head classroom comparisons with (young) adults show:
- similar end-of-course outcomes for PPP and TBLT;
- TBLT advantages in fluency, structural complexity and delayed retention;
- a PPP advantage on test-type proficiency scores.

The best-supported option for this product is a hybrid, "task-supported" design: task or diagnostic first, then brief explicit focus, then practice, then a repeat task. I found no outcome research on TTT as a distinct framework. Its support is practitioner rationale plus the general evidence for focused instruction.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses and methodological critiques [META]**

*Bryfonski & McKay (2019), Language Teaching Research (Sept 2019 issue)*
- First synthesis of long-term, programme-level TBLT implementation in authentic classrooms. Earlier meta-analyses covered single tasks or short treatments.
- 52 studies; overall strong positive effect, **d = 0.93**, across a variety of learning outcomes.
- Sources: [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168817744389); [ERIC EJ1225836](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1225836)
- The four moderators B&M reported were programme region, institution type, needs analysis and cycles of implementation. — as summarised in [Xuan, Cheung & Liu](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127)

*Xuan, Cheung & Liu (LTR, online 2022, issue 2025): technical comment plus new meta-analysis*
- Identifies three flaws in B&M: (1) loose inclusion criteria, (2) oversimplified outcome effect-size calculation, (3) neglect of moderators.
- Corrected estimate: **g = 0.61**, "a more modest effect".
- In meta-regression, only **sample size** and **teacher-as-researcher** accounted for the effect.
- Sources: [SAGE (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365021726_How_effective_is_task-based_language_teaching_to_enhance_second_language_learning_A_technical_comment_on_Bryfonski_and_McKay_2019)

*Other critiques*
- **Boers, Bryfonski, Faez & McKay (2021)**, SSLA 43, 2–24, "A call for cautious interpretation of meta-analytic reviews". The outcome of a meta-analysis depends on how effect sizes are calculated, how factors are defined and which studies are included; the paper re-examines three published meta-analyses. It won the Albert Valdman award for best SSLA article of 2021. Note that Bryfonski and McKay are co-authors. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/call-for-cautious-interpretation-of-metaanalytic-reviews/76C39A1C16E34AEA928E69FF89F69E72); [ERIC EJ1291561](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1291561)
- **Boers & Faez (2023)**, LTR, "Meta-analysis to estimate the relative effectiveness of TBLT programs: Are we there yet?" The title questions whether meta-analysis can yet establish TBLT's relative effectiveness; the arguments themselves were not read. [verify] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688231167573)

*Input vs output instruction*
- **Shintani, Li & Ellis (2013)**, *Language Learning* 63(2), 296–329, compared comprehension-based (input) with production-based (output/practice) grammar instruction: 35 direct comparisons from 30 studies.
  - Both had **large effects on receptive and productive knowledge**.
  - Comprehension-based instruction was better for receptive knowledge within one week, but the difference diminished over time.
  - Sources: [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12001); [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/2911665/Shintani_N_Li_S_and_Ellis_R_2013_Comprehension_based_versus_production_based_instruction_A_meta_analysis_of_comparative_studies_Language_Learning_63_296_329)

**Direct comparisons and component studies [EXP]**

*Harris & Leeming (2022), Journal of Second Language Studies 5(1), 114–143*
- Four intact classes of Japanese university students on a one-year oral English course were randomly assigned to TBLT or PPP.
- Speaking proficiency and speaking self-efficacy were measured three times during the year.
- **Both conditions grew in proficiency and self-efficacy, with no difference in final outcomes.** Interviews explored students' views of each approach.
- Sources: [John Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jsls.20014.har); [JBE platform](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/jsls.20014.har)

*PPP vs TBLT vs the Production-Oriented Approach (POA), The Asia-Pacific Education Researcher (2023)*
- 343 Chinese university freshmen and 6 teachers in an 18-week pre/post quasi-experiment.
- On English proficiency test scores (ANCOVA), **PPP showed a moderate effect-size advantage over TBLT**.
- POA outperformed both on vocabulary and writing.
- Source: [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40299-023-00728-y)

*de la Fuente (2006), LTR 10, 263–295*
- University learners of Spanish; one traditional PPP vocabulary lesson compared with two task-based lessons.
- Findings:
  - Lesson type made **no difference to immediate retrieval**.
  - **Task-based lessons were more effective for retrieval one week later.**
  - A task-based lesson with an explicit focus-on-forms component beat task-only for word morphology.
  - The explicit component "may be more effective when placed at the end of the lesson, when meaning has been acquired".
- Source: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1191/1362168806lr196oa)

*González-Lloret & Nielson (2015), LTR 19(5), 525–549*
- Adult US Border Patrol agents learning Spanish. A new TBLT course was compared with the previous grammar-based course.
- TBLT learners scored **significantly higher on fluency and structural complexity**, and similarly on lexical complexity.
- Current and former students felt TBLT served their professional need-to-know Spanish.
- Sources: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168814541745); [ERIC EJ1072232](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1072232)

*Spada, Jessop, Tomita, Suzuki & Valeo (2014), LTR 18(4), 453–473*
- Adult ESL learners received 12 hours of instruction on the passive in one of two formats:
  - **integrated** FFI: attention to form embedded in communicative practice;
  - **isolated** FFI: form work separated from communicative practice.
- There were **no significant differences** between the groups over time on a written grammar test or an oral communication task.
- Sources: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168813519883); [ERIC EJ1041298](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1041298)

*Ellis, Li & Zhu (System, published online 2018)*
- 72 eighth-grade EFL learners were randomly assigned to "explicit instruction (EI) + task" or "task only". Both groups did oral dictogloss tasks eliciting the past passive.
- The EI group had "a brief grammar lesson on the English passive voice followed by 5 min of practice" before the task.
- Pre-task EI produced **more frequent but not more accurate** use of the target structure, and had **detrimental global effects** on complexity, accuracy and fluency (a trade-off).
- Sources: [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X18305190); [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/37637770/Ellis_R_Li_S_and_Zhu_Y_2018_The_effects_of_pre_task_explicit_instruction_on_the_performance_of_a_focused_task_System_Published_online)

*Li, Ellis & Zhu (2016), Annual Review of Applied Linguistics 36, 205–229, "Task-based versus task-supported language instruction"*
- 150 Chinese middle-school learners, English passive, a 2-hour treatment of two dictogloss tasks.
- Five groups: control; task only; explicit instruction before the task; within-task feedback; explicit instruction plus feedback.
- The results could not be retrieved (see Gaps).
- Source: [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/abs/taskbased-versus-tasksupported-language-instruction-an-experimental-study/ED9E4C94BAAC5B71584F503C88878CDB)

**Reviews and practitioner positions [REVIEW / PRACT]**

*Anderson (2017), ELT Journal 71(2), "A potted history of PPP with the help of ELT Journal"*
- Charts PPP's history, the heavy criticism it received in the 1990s, and its close link to coursebook syllabi.
- Argues that recent SLA evidence supporting explicit, practice-oriented instruction means PPP "can no longer be rejected as incompatible with research evidence", and that it "may be enjoying a revival".
- Sources: [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/eltj/article-abstract/71/2/218/2447419); [ERIC EJ1142725](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1142725)

*Opinion from a practitioner comparison paper* (not outcome data) [verify attribution]
- PPP is effective for preparing students for exams, while TBLT accommodates real-life communicative purposes, so teachers should choose by learning goal.
- TBLT makes the teacher a facilitator.
- Source: [ResearchGate: PPP vs TBLT, comparison of two teaching templates](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375642779_PRESENTATION_PRACTICE_PRODUCTION_VS_TASK-BASED_LANGUAGE_TEACHING_A_COMPARISON_OF_TWO_TEACHING_TEMPLATES)

*British Council on TTT*
- TTT suits intermediate+ learners who have met the language before, and mixed-level groups. — [British Council: Test teach test](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/article/test-teach-test)

### Inferences
- **No framework "wins" outright; the evidence supports task-supported hybrids.**
  - Both PPP and TBLT grow proficiency and self-efficacy (Harris & Leeming 2022).
  - TBLT's edge shows up in outcomes this product cares about most: spoken fluency and complexity (González-Lloret & Nielson 2015) and delayed retention (de la Fuente 2006).
  - PPP's edge shows up on test-type scores (APER 2023).
  - Isolated and integrated form focus are equivalent for adults (Spada et al. 2014).
- **Proposed decision rule for adult Russian-speaking A2–B1 learners:**
  - **Short PPP ("straight arrow")** when an item is new, or its concept is absent from or different in Russian: present perfect vs past simple, articles, some future forms, aspect-driven choices. The British Council says concept checking is "particularly valuable where a concept does not exist, or is different in the mother tongue"; see Q4. Keep presentation to a 1–2-minute clip plus CCQs, then practise immediately.
  - **TTT / boomerang (the default)** when the learner has probably met the form, which fits the typical "understand but can't speak" profile. Use a spoken diagnostic, then clarify only the gaps.
  - **Willis task cycle** for fluency, confidence and functional lessons (small talk, ordering, complaining, storytelling, work situations). At A2, add pre-task phrase support. Put the explicit language focus after the task, as in Willis and de la Fuente (2006).
- **Do not front-load grammar before a fluency task.** Ellis, Li & Zhu show that even brief pre-task explicit instruction increases use of the form but can depress overall complexity, accuracy and fluency. In fluency-goal lessons, give the explanation after the first attempt, or limit it to one pattern.
- **Include both form focus and meaning-focused use in every lesson.** Order matters less than inclusion (Spada et al. 2014; Scrivener's "all three aspects in every lesson").
- **Describe TBLT's evidence as a "moderate" advantage (g ≈ 0.6), not a "large" one**, in both methodology and marketing claims. The d = 0.93 figure is contested, including by its own authors.

### Gaps
- **Li, Ellis & Zhu (2016) results.** The results of this cleanest task-based vs task-supported contrast could not be retrieved.
- **IRAL study.** Results were not retrieved for "Speaking proficiency development in EFL classrooms: measuring the differential effect of TBLT and PPP teaching approaches" ([De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2022-0082/html)).
- **Conflicting summary of Harris & Leeming.** A search-engine summary claimed that Harris & Leeming (2022) showed "immediate task performance benefits" for PPP and "more substantial language development" from TBLT's post-task language focus. This conflicts with their abstract ("no difference in final outcomes") and could not be traced to a source. Rely on the abstract.
- **Boers & Faez (2023).** Arguments not read.
- **TTT outcomes.** No outcome studies of TTT as a distinct framework were found.
- **Long's TBLT.** Long's TBLT (focus on form, needs analysis) was not retrieved in this session.
- **Ellis's task criteria.** Ellis's criteria for what counts as a "task" were not verified: primary focus on meaning, a gap, learners' own resources, and a clearly defined outcome (from memory).
- **Technology-mediated and AI settings.** No RCTs were found comparing lesson frameworks in AI or voice one-to-one tutoring. ## 3. Explicit vs implicit instruction (Norris & Ortega 2000; Spada & Tomita 2010; Goo et al. 2015; Kang, Sok & Han 2019): effect sizes, durability, and what "explicit" should look like in practice

### Takeaway
Across three decades of meta-analyses, form-focused instruction has large effects. The earlier syntheses found explicit instruction clearly ahead of implicit instruction:
- Norris & Ortega (2000): d ≈ 1.13 vs 0.54.
- Spada & Tomita (2010): the explicit advantage held for both simple and complex features and extended to spontaneous use.

The most recent broad synthesis narrows the gap:
- Kang et al. (2019): overall g = 1.06, with only a "minor" difference between explicit and implicit instruction;
- secondary reports say implicit instruction is ahead at delayed posttests.

Outcome-measure type and proficiency moderate the effects. Practical conclusion: use brief explicit input embedded in meaningful, repeated oral practice, with recycling over time to secure durability.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses [META]**

*Norris & Ortega (2000), Language Learning 50(3), 417–528*
- 49 unique-sample studies published 1980–1998.
- Findings:
  - focused L2 instruction produces "large target-oriented gains";
  - "explicit types of instruction are more effective than implicit types";
  - Focus on Form and Focus on Forms interventions produce "equivalent and large effects".
- Sources: [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0023-8333.00136); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effectiveness-of-L2-Instruction:-A-Research-and-Norris-Ortega/6dfc0158e7aa0cb226a9d4130018b0a9b474d795)
- Average effects: **explicit d = 1.13 vs implicit d = 0.54**, i.e. explicit worked roughly twice as well on the posttests used. [verify: secondary summary] — [LanguagePlans: why pop-up grammar beats grammar units](https://languageplans.com/pedagogy/why-pop-up-grammar-beats-grammar-units)
- Durability: secondary summaries describe explicit gains as "larger, more durable". [verify against N&O's delayed-posttest tables] — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228003219_Effectiveness_of_L2_Instruction_A_Research_Synthesis_and_Quantitative_Meta-analysis)

*Spada & Tomita (2010), Language Learning 60(2), 263–308*
- 41 studies. English grammatical features were coded as simple or complex by the number of criteria (rules) needed to arrive at the correct form (Hulstijn & de Graaff 1994). Instruction types were coded following N&O.
- Explicit instruction produced **larger effect sizes than implicit instruction for both simple and complex features**.
- It "positively contributes to learners' controlled knowledge **and spontaneous use**" of both simple and complex forms.
- Sources: [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x); [ERIC EJ883419](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ883419)

*Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella (2015), in Rebuschat (Ed.),* Implicit and Explicit Learning of Languages*, 443–483 (John Benjamins); an update of N&O*
- 34 studies directly comparing explicit and implicit treatments: 11 from N&O plus 23 new.
- A **combination of oral and written treatments worked best** for both explicit and implicit instruction.
- Explicit instruction had a medium-to-large effect on **free production**, g = 1.443. [verify]
- Sources: [John Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Implicit-and-explicit-instruction-in-L2-learning-Goo-Granena/aad1a1dfd2eb49451885ed21a4a78075fc5d7c9e)

*Kang, Sok & Han (2019), LTR 23(4), 428–453: 35 years (1980–2015) of form-focused instruction*
- 54 studies, 5,051 learners; overall **g = 1.06 (95% CI 0.84–1.29)**.
- Only "a minor difference" between explicit and implicit instruction.
- Statistically significant moderators: **mode of outcome measure, learners' onset L2 proficiency, research setting, and intensity of instruction**.
- Sources: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168818776671); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326330342_Thirty-five_years_of_ISLA_on_form-focused_instruction_A_meta-analysis)
- Studies citing Kang et al. report effects for both implicit (g = 1.38) and explicit (g = 1.11) instruction at immediate posttest, with **implicit instruction ahead at delayed posttests**; implicit knowledge is said to "outlast" explicit knowledge. [verify: from secondary summaries, not the full text] — [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168818776671)

*Li (2024), Foreign Language Annals*
- "Effects of different forms of explicit instruction on L2 development: A meta-analysis" compares subtypes of explicit instruction. Content not retrieved. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/flan.12726)

**What "explicit" looked like in effective studies [EXP]**
- **Brief is enough to change behaviour.** A brief grammar lesson plus about 5 minutes of practice changed task behaviour: the form was used more often but not more accurately, and global CAF suffered (Ellis, Li & Zhu, System, online 2018). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X18305190)
- **Placement after the task worked.** An explicit focus-on-forms component placed after a meaning-focused task improved acquisition of morphology (de la Fuente 2006). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1191/1362168806lr196oa)
- **Isolated and integrated delivery were equivalent** for adults over 12 hours (Spada et al. 2014). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168813519883)
- **Explicit can be inductive.** In guided induction, learners co-construct the rule by answering guiding questions about input. It produced robust learning and deep processing of a complex structure inside a videogame, without a teacher (Cerezo, Caras & Leow 2016; details in Q4). — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effectiveness-of-guided-induction-versus-deductive-instruction-on-the-development-of-complex-spanish-gustar-structures/0A256FD27F2DF9B1C21C577FA0A24725)

### Inferences
- **Explicit instruction is the evidence-backed default for grammar points** in a 20-minute lesson, given its consistently large effects, including on spontaneous use (Spada & Tomita).
- **Durability depends on what follows the explanation.** Three findings point the same way:
  - Kang et al.'s delayed-test pattern favours implicit instruction;
  - task-based lessons showed a delayed advantage in de la Fuente (2006);
  - the comprehension-based advantage faded over time in Shintani et al.

  All three point to extensive meaning-focused use and spaced recycling across lessons.
- **Operational definition of "explicit" for the app (proposal):**
  1. Show the form in a meaningful context sentence, ideally the learner's own attempt from the diagnostic.
  2. Give a one-sentence rule of thumb, in Russian if the concept is absent in Russian.
  3. Give 2–3 model sentences, including one contrast pair.
  4. Show a timeline or visual for tense and aspect.
  5. Ask 2–4 CCQs.
  6. Move immediately to oral controlled practice with corrective feedback.
  7. Run a communicative task.
  8. Recycle the item in later lessons.
- **Deliver explanations as audio plus on-screen text.** Oral plus written treatments were strongest in Goo et al.
- **Measure spontaneous speech, not only drill accuracy.** Outcome-measure mode moderates effects in Kang et al., and explicit instruction looks best on explicit-knowledge tests. App analytics should track accuracy of the target form in free talk.

### Gaps
- **Spada & Tomita numbers.** Exact d values for simple vs complex features and for controlled vs free-production measures were not retrievable.
- **Goo et al. overall values.** The overall explicit vs implicit g values could not be verified.
- **N&O delayed posttests.** The delayed-posttest effect sizes were not verified.
- **Li (2024, FLAN).** Findings by subtype (rule explanation, metalinguistic information, deductive vs inductive, etc.) were not read.
- **Unidentified meta-analysis.** A "meta-analysis of comparative studies" of explicit vs implicit instruction was seen but its authors and results were not identified: [ResearchGate 347408013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347408013_Explicit_Versus_Implicit_Instruction_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Comparative_Studies).
- **Proficiency moderator.** Kang et al. found learners' starting proficiency to be a significant moderator, but I could not retrieve which way it runs, i.e. whether explicit instruction helps lower- or higher-proficiency learners more.
- **Possibly relevant arXiv paper.** An arXiv paper titled "Getting Explicit Instruction Right" ([arXiv 2506.10480](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10480)) surfaced in results but was not read.

## 4. How do expert teachers clarify meaning and form (CCQs, timelines, guided discovery, contextualised examples, contrast pairs)? Noticing and input enhancement; judicious use of the learner's L1

### Takeaway
Practitioner consensus (British Council, CELTA):
- establish meaning through context first;
- check it with concept-checking questions, never "Do you understand?";
- use timelines as an aid to explanation, not as a check.

Theory (Schmidt's noticing hypothesis) and evidence agree that learners must attend to the form:
- merely highlighting forms has a small effect (d = 0.22);
- guided induction with guiding questions produces robust learning, even when fully automated.

For adults, using the L1 to convey meaning is supported by an experiment (Lee & Macaro 2013: adults learned more vocabulary with teacher L1 use) and a 2026 meta-analysis (g ≈ 1.01; largest for speaking and for tertiary learners). The studies are heterogeneous.

### Cited Findings
**CCQs and timelines [PRACT]**
- The British Council's guidance on concept checking:
  - It avoids asking "Do you understand?", which can be answered "yes" without real understanding.
  - Ways to concept-check: concept questions, realia, asking learners to repeat instructions, learners explaining meaning, and open-class questions.
  - Timelines help establish tenses, but they "are not a substitute for concept questions … They are aids to explanation, but do not necessarily check understanding". Concept questions can be used to elicit a timeline from learners.
  - Concept questions are "particularly valuable where a concept does not exist, or is different in the mother tongue (e.g. the perfect aspect, ways of expressing the future)", and for culturally loaded items. They also raise awareness of association and connotation, and draw attention to collocations and fixed expressions.
  - Sources: [British Council TeachingEnglish: Concept checking](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/c/concept-checking); [British Council TeachingEnglish: Checking understanding](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/managing-lesson/checking-understanding)
- CELTA's clarification criterion covers meaning, form and phonology "to an appropriate degree/depth" (see Q1). Trainers teach a step-by-step MFP clarification. — [DC Teacher Training](https://www.dcteachertraining.com/post/how-to-clarify-meaning-form-pronunciation-celta). A widely used trainer guide to writing CCQs exists but was not read: [Sandy Millin: How to write CCQs](https://sandymillin.wordpress.com/2020/07/20/how-to-write-ccqs-concept-checking-questions/)

**Noticing and input enhancement [REVIEW / META]**
- **Schmidt (1990)**, *Applied Linguistics* 11(2), 129–158: subliminal language learning is impossible, and noticing is "the necessary and sufficient condition for converting input to intake".
  - Schmidt (1995): "what learners notice in input is what becomes intake for learning".
  - Later, weaker version: "people learn about the things they attend to and do not learn much about the things they do not attend to".
  - Sources: [Schmidt 1990 (PDF, NFLRC)](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/PDFs/SCHMIDT%20The%20role%20of%20consciousness%20in%20second%20language%20learning.pdf); [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/11/2/129/163482); [Wikipedia: Noticing hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noticing_hypothesis)
- **Lee & Huang (2008)**, *SSLA* 30(3), meta-analysis of visual (textual) input enhancement: 16 studies (20 samples).
  - Readers given enhanced texts "barely outperformed" those given unenhanced texts: **d = 0.22**.
  - The authors caution that divergent methods in the primary studies may explain the small effect.
  - Sources: [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/visual-input-enhancement-and-grammar-learning-a-metaanalytic-review/B9D0C50B09928C20C94548B37B29A042); [ERIC full text citing it](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1134557.pdf)

**Guided discovery (inductive) vs deductive presentation [EXP]**
- **Cerezo, Caras & Leow (2016)**, *SSLA*: 70 English-speaking beginning learners of Spanish in three groups.
  - Guided induction means learners co-construct rules, with attention directed to relevant aspects of the input and guiding questions asked.
  - It "can successfully promote robust learning outcomes and deep processing" of complex Spanish *gustar* structures.
  - It was **implemented in a videogame "where teacher mediation is not possible"**.
  - Sources: [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effectiveness-of-guided-induction-versus-deductive-instruction-on-the-development-of-complex-spanish-gustar-structures/0A256FD27F2DF9B1C21C577FA0A24725); [ERIC EJ1103508](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1103508)
- Earlier college-level comparisons of guided-inductive and deductive teaching exist, but their results were not retrieved:
  - Haight, Herron & Cole (2007), elementary level ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227719672_The_Effects_of_Deductive_and_Guided_Inductive_Instructional_Approaches_on_the_Learning_of_Grammar_in_the_Elementary_Foreign_Language_College_Classroom));
  - Vogel et al. (2011), intermediate French ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229937794_Effectiveness_of_a_Guided_Inductive_Versus_a_Deductive_Approach_on_the_Learning_of_Grammar_in_the_Intermediate-Level_College_French_Classroom)).

**The learner's L1 / translanguaging [META / EXP / REVIEW]**
- **Multilevel Bayesian meta-analysis of translanguaging pedagogy** (*Language Teaching*, June 2026):
  - 108 effect sizes from 40 independent studies, 3,145 learners;
  - pooled **g = 1.014 (95% credible interval 0.748–1.291)**;
  - **speaking** had the largest effect of all skill domains;
  - effects were **larger among tertiary-level learners**;
  - target language, treatment duration and linguistic distance moderated effects only weakly.
  - Source: [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/multilevel-bayesian-metaanalysis-of-the-effects-of-translanguaging-pedagogy-on-l2-achievement/552777E82223C539135A50D9B1DAADB4)
- **Systematic review of translanguaging and L2 reading** (*Languages*, 2025):
  - small but significant positive effect, **g = 0.33 (CI 0.21–0.45)**, with considerable variation;
  - quantitative findings are mixed: 10 studies positive, 6 with no significant effect;
  - adult advanced readers show random or L2-favouring preferences for L1 vs L2 resources, i.e. L1 reliance declines with proficiency. [verify attribution of the last point]
  - Source: [MDPI Languages](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages10080200)
- **Lee & Macaro (2013)**, *Modern Language Journal* 97, 887–901: Korean EFL learners, 443 primary children and 286 university adults with higher proficiency.
  - After two week-long instructional sessions, **both age groups learned more vocabulary with teacher L1 (Korean) use than with English-only instruction**, on both form recall and form recognition.
  - Young learners benefited more.
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2013.12044.x); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Investigating-Age-in-the-Use-of-L1-or-English-Only-Lee-Macaro/aae03f4e3e0414a7443052b2fe7d76ac81cc494a)
- **Hall & Cook (2012)**, *Language Teaching* 45(3), 271–308, state-of-the-art review:
  - documents continuing widespread use of students' own language in classrooms worldwide, and a revival of support for it;
  - reviews support from psycholinguistic/cognitive, general-learning and sociocultural frameworks;
  - reviews studies of the extent and functions of own-language use, and of teacher and learner attitudes;
  - discusses how to implement a bilingual approach.
  - Sources: [King's College London](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/own-language-use-in-language-teaching-and-learning); [ERIC EJ974803](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ974803)
  - Companion British Council survey (not read): [Hall & Cook (2013), Own-language use in ELT: exploring global practices and attitudes](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/sites/teacheng/files/C448%20Own%20Language%20use%20in%20ELT_A4_FINAL_WEB%20ONLY_0.pdf)

### Inferences
**CCQ procedure for the AI (proposal built on the British Council guidance)**
1. For each target item, the methodologist stores 2–4 "concept components". Example, present perfect of experience: past event + time not stated or unimportant + "in my life up to now".
2. The AI asks one closed CCQ per component (yes/no or either/or), in language simpler than the target item. Example for "She's been to Paris": "Did she go to Paris in the past? (Yes) Do we know when? (No) Is *when* important here? (No)".
3. Never ask "Do you understand?".
4. Finish with a personalised production check, e.g. "Tell me one thing you've never done".
5. If an answer is wrong, switch to a different explanation route (contrast pair → Russian contrast → timeline) rather than replaying the same clip.

**Timelines**
- Show them on screen during the audio clip, then always follow with CCQs. The British Council says a timeline aids explanation but does not check understanding.

**Guided discovery for half-known items (B1)**
1. Show 3–4 contextualised example sentences, with the form highlighted and read aloud.
2. Ask 2 guiding questions, e.g. "Which sentences say *when*? Which don't?"
3. The learner states the rule; Russian is acceptable.
4. The AI confirms with a one-sentence rule and a contrast pair.
5. Ask CCQs.

Highlighting alone is weak (d = 0.22), so always pair it with guiding questions. Cerezo et al. show guided induction can work fully automated. For A2 learners, or conceptually new items, use a short deductive clip instead, which costs less time and cognitive load (see Q5).

**Contrast pairs as the main meaning device for Russian speakers**
- Example: "I lived in Moscow for five years" (no longer) vs "I've lived in Moscow for five years" (still do).
- This follows the British Council's point that concepts absent in, or different from, the L1 are the prime case for concept work. It is a practitioner-derived proposal; no outcome study of contrast pairs was retrieved.

**L1 (Russian) policy (proposal)**
- Use Russian for:
  - concept explanations of items that do not map onto Russian (articles, perfect aspect, some modals and futures);
  - quick glosses of abstract or low-frequency words;
  - rescuing breakdowns in instructions;
  - the learner's own rule formulation in guided discovery.
- Keep all practice, task language and feedback reformulations in English.
- Fade Russian from A2 to B1.
- Support: adults learned more vocabulary with teacher L1 use (Lee & Macaro 2013). The 2026 meta-analysis found the largest translanguaging effects for speaking and tertiary learners. The reading evidence is smaller (g = 0.33), and L1 preference declines with proficiency.

### Gaps
- **CCQs and timelines.** No empirical outcome studies were found. Their support is practitioner consensus.
- **Rule-writing guidance.** Two practitioner sources could not be verified in this session (search quota exhausted): Swan's (1994) design criteria for pedagogic rules (truth, demarcation, clarity, simplicity, conceptual parsimony, relevance; from memory) and Thornbury's guidance in *How to Teach Grammar*. Verify before citing.
- **L1 vs L2 glosses.** Kim, Lee & Lee's meta-analysis in LTR (dated 2024 in the search result; the DOI suggests online-first around 2020–21) was not read: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168820981394).
- **Learner attitudes to L1.** Macaro & Lee (2013, *TESOL Quarterly*), on attitudes to L1 use by learner age, was not read: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tesq.74).
- **Monolingual vs multilingual teaching.** Brown (LTR, 2023, "Monolingual versus multilingual foreign language teaching: French and Arabic at beginning levels") was not read: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168821990347).
- **Counter-evidence.** A search summary mentioned a narrative review finding no advantage of translanguaging over English-only for vocabulary, grammar and metalinguistic awareness. It could not be attributed to a source.

## 5. What is the ideal length and format of explanations for adults (cognitive load theory, worked examples, micro-lessons), and how short can an explanation be before practice?

### Takeaway
No retrieved study pins down an optimal or minimum length for a grammar explanation. The converging evidence points to very short, segmented, worked-example-based explanations followed immediately by practice:
- **Cognitive load theory:** working memory is limited; novices learn best from explicit worked examples, which lose value as expertise grows (the expertise reversal effect).
- **Engagement:** video engagement drops sharply beyond about 6 minutes.
- **Segmenting:** supported in Mayer's own experiments but non-significant in a 2025 meta-analysis.
- **Dose:** in an SLA study, a brief lesson plus about 5 minutes of practice already changed performance.

### Cited Findings
**Cognitive load theory [REVIEW]**
- CLT assumes a limited working memory, with partly independent processing channels for visual and auditory information, interacting with an unlimited long-term memory. Instruction should be designed so that working memory is available for learning. [verify attribution] — [Sweller: Cognitive load theory and teaching English (TESL Ontario PDF)](https://contact.teslontario.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Sweller-CognitiveLoad.pdf)
- Worked examples are most effective for novices, who lack stored problem-solving knowledge structures.
  - **Expertise reversal effect:** heavy use of worked examples becomes less effective, then redundant or counter-productive, as expertise increases.
  - Teaching should match expertise: "novice learners learn best from clear, explicit instructions (such as worked examples)", while more expert learners benefit from more independent problem solving.
  - Source: [NSW CESE (2017), Cognitive load theory: Research that teachers really need to understand](https://education.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/main-education/about-us/educational-data/cese/2017-cognitive-load-theory.pdf)
- A (lower-quality) application to teaching tenses lists techniques for reducing extraneous load: worked examples, and the split-attention, goal-free, modality and redundancy effects. — [Academia.edu: Applying CLT in teaching tenses](https://www.academia.edu/75873266/Applying_Cognitive_Load_Theory_in_Teaching_Tenses_for_Second_Language_Learners)
- A *Cambridge Handbook of Working Memory and Language* chapter on CLT and instructional design for language learning exists but was not read. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-working-memory-and-language/cognitive-load-theory-and-instructional-design-for-language-learning/C1A61C6FC46C173594554509664B2757)

**Video length and segmenting [EXP / META]**
- **Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014)**, *ACM Learning@Scale*, pp. 41–50: 6.9 million video-watching sessions across four edX courses.
  - Video length was "by far the most significant indicator of engagement". Students rarely made it through videos longer than 9 minutes.
  - The authors recommend videos of **6 minutes or less**, and pre-production planning to segment content into chunks under 6 minutes.
  - Caveat: this measures engagement, not learning, and the courses were not language courses.
  - Sources: [Guo et al. 2014 (PDF)](https://learningatscale.acm.org/las2014/talks/paper_philip_guo2.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262393281_How_video_production_affects_student_engagement_An_empirical_study_of_MOOC_videos)
- **Mayer's segmenting and pre-training principles** (*Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*, ch. 13, pp. 316–344; edition and authorship not verified):
  - **Segmenting:** people learn more deeply from learner-paced segments than from one continuous presentation. Supported in 10 of 10 experiments, median **d = 0.79**.
  - **Pre-training:** people learn more deeply when they already know the names and characteristics of key concepts. Supported in 13 of 16 experiments, median **d = 0.75**.
  - Sources: [Chapter PDF](https://edtechuvic.ca/edci337/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2022/09/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pre-training-and-modality-principles.pdf); [Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258/9781139547369c13_p316-344_CBO.pdf/principles_for_managing_essential_processing_in_multimedia_learning_segmenting_pretraining_and_modality_principles.pdf)
- **Contradicting evidence:** a 2025 meta-analysis of Mayer's multimedia research (*Educational Research Review*) found **non-significant** effects for:
  - segmenting (g = 0.19);
  - pre-training (g = 0.28);
  - animation, voice, immersion, avoiding redundant captions, contiguity and social presence.
  - Sources: [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X25000673); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395601295_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Richard_Mayer's_Multimedia_Learning_Research_Searching_for_Boundary_Conditions_of_Design_Principles_Across_Multiple_Media_Types)

**SLA-specific signals on explanation dose and format [EXP / META]**
- A **brief** grammar lesson plus about **5 minutes of practice** before a task was enough to raise use of the form, but it came with global CAF costs (Ellis, Li & Zhu, System, online 2018). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X18305190)
- **Oral plus written** treatments were most effective for both explicit and implicit instruction (Goo et al. 2015). — [John Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo)

### Inferences
**Explanation-clip specification (proposal)**
- One learning point per clip.
- Clip length 30–90 seconds, with a hard ceiling of about 2 minutes (far below the ~6-minute engagement threshold).
- Total explanation time of at most about 2–4 minutes per 20-minute lesson, so that at least 75% of the time is learner speech. The audience's problem is speaking, not understanding.
- After every clip, an AI interaction (CCQ or drill) happens before the next clip plays. This is a learner-paced "segment → interact" loop, consistent with segmenting, even though evidence for segmenting is mixed.
- The learner can replay a clip or switch to a Russian version.

**Clip template**
1. A 1–2-line mini-dialogue that sets the context.
2. Meaning in one sentence, with a timeline or contrast pair.
3. The form pattern on screen, with 2–3 worked examples (including negative and question forms if needed).
4. A pronunciation model (contractions, weak forms), repeated twice.
5. A handover line, e.g. "Now let's check".

**Adjusting by level and format**
- **Fade support by level** (expertise reversal):
  - A2: full worked examples and a deductive rule;
  - B1: guided discovery (Q4) and fewer examples.
- **Pre-teach key chunks and vocabulary before tasks**, as in the Willis pre-task and Mayer's pre-training principle, but treat the evidence for pre-training as mixed.
- **Show audio and text together** (Goo et al.). Keep the on-screen text minimal (pattern plus examples, not a transcript) to respect redundancy concerns.

### Gaps
- **Explanation length.** No SLA study was retrieved that manipulates explanation length (e.g. 1 vs 3 vs 10 minutes) against learning outcomes. "How short is too short" remains unanswered empirically.
- **Micro-learning.** No meta-analysis of micro-learning or micro-lessons in language learning was retrieved (search quota exhausted).
- **Redundancy vs dual mode.** The tension between Mayer's redundancy principle (avoid on-screen text that duplicates narration) and the L2 evidence favouring oral plus written input could not be resolved with L2-specific multimedia studies.
- **Guo et al. applicability.** The findings are MOOC engagement data, not learning outcomes, and do not involve language learners.

## 6. Lead-in / warm-up: purpose (activating schemata, personalisation, motivation); how top teachers open a lesson and build rapport quickly

### Takeaway
In CELTA and British Council practice, the lead-in is a short, topic-linked opening. It:
- generates interest;
- activates learners' existing knowledge (schemata);
- personalises the topic;
- sets up the context from which the target language will emerge.

It is distinct from a generic "warmer". Its support is practitioner consensus. The main empirical link between teachers' motivational behaviour and learner motivation comes from classroom-observation research (Guilloteaux & Dörnyei 2008), whose effect sizes could not be verified in this session.

### Cited Findings
**Practitioner consensus [PRACT]**
- **Lead-in purpose.** A lead-in is designed to "warm the students up": to generate interest and get them thinking about the topic. It prepares them for the new language or skill by activating background knowledge.
  - Introducing a topic (pictures, video, questions) prompts a mental expectation built from existing knowledge, a *schema*, "so we can say that the aim of a lead-in stage is to 'activate your students' schemata'".
  - Learners' existing knowledge and experience "can then be used to personalise the lesson".
  - [verify page attribution among these three] — [ESLbase: How to plan a TEFL lesson](https://www.eslbase.com/tefl-a-z/lesson-planning/); [MyEnglishPages: warmers and lead-ins](https://www.myenglishpages.com/warmers-and-lead-ins-as-presentation-techniques/); [ELT Notebook: warm-ups and lead-ins](https://eltnotebook.blogspot.com/2016/03/an-elt-glossary-warm-ups-and-lead-ins.html)
- **Warmer vs lead-in.** The two are often confused in practice; a lead-in prepares for the specific new language or skill. About 5 minutes of warming up and leading in is suggested for a typical classroom lesson. **Empirical [EXP, observational]**
- **Guilloteaux & Dörnyei (2008)**, *TESOL Quarterly* 42(1), 55–77: a large classroom-oriented investigation of whether teachers' motivational strategies relate to student motivation.
  - Setting: 40 ESOL classrooms in South Korea, 27 teachers, more than 1,300 learners.
  - Measures: a student self-report questionnaire; the MOLT observation scheme (real-time coding of observable teacher behaviours derived from Dörnyei's 2001 motivational-strategies framework, modelled on the COLT scheme); and a post-hoc observer rating scale.
  - The study was framed as addressing the lack of empirical evidence for the widely held belief that motivational strategies enhance student motivation.
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2008.tb00207.x); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Motivating-Language-Learners:-A-Classroom-Oriented-Guilloteaux-D%C3%B6rnyei/bfb6c05532435fbd6cc7e3f9a2af1a4a0da02659)

### Inferences
**Opening procedure for a 20-minute voice lesson (about 60–90 s; proposal)**
1. Greet the learner by name and call back to the last session, e.g. "Last time you told me about your trip to Kazan…". This gives continuity and rapport, and requires learner memory in the app.
2. Ask one personal, topic-linked question the learner can answer comfortably. This activates schemata, personalises the lesson and gives an early success moment.
3. State the goal as a real-life can-do outcome, e.g. "By the end you'll be able to talk about your experience in a job interview". This makes the purpose clear, as in Willis's "clarify the outcome".
4. Hand over to the diagnostic task, so the lead-in doubles as the TTT "test" (see Q1).

**Other opening choices**
- **Merge lead-in and context-setting.** The lead-in question should come from the situation in which the target language naturally occurs (CELTA 2c). For example, "Have you ever…?" small talk leads directly into present perfect of experience.
- **Avoid generic warmers that are unrelated to the lesson aim.** In a 20-minute lesson they consume scarce speaking time; practitioner sources distinguish them from lead-ins.

**Rapport behaviours to specify in the AI prompt** (practitioner-derived design choices, not tested interventions)
- Use the learner's name.
- Respond to the content of the learner's answers (a genuine follow-up question) before any correction.
- Keep the first exchange easy.
- Praise specific achievements.
- Use Russian only when the learner is stuck.

### Gaps
- **Guilloteaux & Dörnyei result.** Their quantitative result could not be verified. From memory and unverified, they reported significant positive correlations between teachers' motivational practice and learners' motivated behaviour. Check the abstract before citing any coefficient.
- **Dörnyei's strategies.** Dörnyei's (2001) catalogue of motivational strategies, including those for lesson openings, was not retrieved.
- **Lead-in experiments.** No experiments were found that isolate the effect of a lead-in or schema-activation stage on adult EFL speaking outcomes. Classic schema-theory studies from reading research (e.g. Carrell) were not retrieved.
- **AI rapport research.** No research was found on rapport-building specifically by AI or voice tutors. A multi-stakeholder study of voice chatbots for English speaking practice surfaced but was not read: [arXiv 2601.19304](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.19304).
- **CELTA rapport criterion.** The wording and number of the CELTA criterion on rapport and learner involvement were not verified.
